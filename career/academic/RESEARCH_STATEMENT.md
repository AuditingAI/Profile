## The problem I work on

An auditor returning to the same client in a second or third year does not begin from nothing. Last
year's number is already there, and it exerts a pull. Anchoring-and-adjustment predicts that the
prior figure becomes the starting point for this year's judgment and that adjustment away from it is
systematically insufficient. This is not a marginal effect in a recurring engagement — it is the
central condition under which most professional review work is actually performed.

My research asks two questions in sequence. First: **which organisational practices actually
disrupt that pull, and through what mechanism?** Second, and this is where the work is going:
**what happens to that mechanism when an AI system produces the anchor?**

## What I have established

My qualifying study developed and fielded a measurement model of anchoring in long-term audit
engagements: eleven constructs, fifty-five items, sixteen hypotheses. Eight organisational
interventions — training and awareness, auditor rotation, analytical tools, structured processes,
feedback and reflection, independent review, regulatory guidance, and performance incentives —
operate through two mediating mechanisms, the quality of the auditor's judgment and the rigour of
the surrounding process, toward one outcome: judgments driven by current evidence rather than the
prior-period figure. The instrument received IRB approval and was fielded with a fully specified
analytic protocol from exploratory factor analysis through structural modelling.

The study reached feasibility rather than validation, and the reason turned out to be the most
useful thing it produced. Applying the eligibility criteria to a commercial research panel of
334,976 participants returned approximately **twenty eligible people** — a prevalence near six per
hundred thousand. Neither compensation nor instrument design was the constraint: effective
compensation ran well above typical academic-survey rates, attention-check failures among eligible
respondents were zero, and every usable respondent supplied a substantive, domain-fluent account of
anchoring in their own work. The constraint was arithmetic, and it was visible on the platform's
configuration screen before any money was committed.

That finding is now a manuscript in preparation. Its contribution is procedural and, I think,
broadly useful: **panel size is close to uninformative for specialist populations; the operative
quantity is eligible prevalence under the study's own screeners, and it is observable in advance at
no cost.** Colleagues designing studies of auditors, clinicians, examiners, or any other narrowly
defined profession are currently budgeting against assumptions this result falsifies.

I regard the honest reporting of that failure as part of the contribution rather than an apology
for it. My faculty assessment described the treatment of it as demonstrating sound research
judgment, and I would rather establish that habit early than acquire it late.

## Where the work is going

The validated human-side model was designed to be extended, and the extension is the dissertation.
When an AI system enters recurring review work, I argue that three effects compound:

**The system output becomes the anchor.** Unlike a prior-year figure, it is generated automatically,
continuously, and at scale, and it typically arrives before the reviewer has formed an independent
view. Anchoring research gives us good reason to expect this; what it does not yet give us is
expert-population evidence at the effect sizes that matter for professional practice.

**The model confirms rather than challenges.** Language models tend to agree with a position the
user has already stated. I frame this deliberately as a *mechanism* rather than an artifact:
sycophancy arises from optimising on human approval, and that optimisation pressure persists across
model generations even as each generation is patched against its current symptoms. A dissertation
that describes 2026 model behaviour and defends in 2028 would be obsolete on arrival; a dissertation
about the underlying pressure would not.

**Successive models converge on each other.** As models reprocess work earlier models produced,
conclusions drift toward one another rather than toward the evidence. Recent work on cross-model
peer review has begun to instrument exactly this.

The consequence I find most significant for governance is this: **recurring professional judgment
can degrade while every individual model passes its own validation.** No component malfunctions. The
degradation lives in the sequence between the model and the human, and in the loop between models —
and neither is inspected by any validation plan I have seen in practice or in the literature.

I am explicit about where this argument is weakest. The evidence for model-to-model convergence
concerns training corpora, not professionals reading model output. The step from "models degrade on
synthetic data" to "auditors converge because the models they consult converge" is an inference, not
a finding. That gap is precisely where my empirical contribution lies: it is a tractable design —
vary whether reviewers see model output and whose output they see, measure dispersion of conclusions
— and to my knowledge nobody has run it. There is also credible evidence pointing the other way, of
AI assistance improving judgment in some tasks, and any serious treatment has to engage it rather
than route around it.

## How I would contribute to a department

**Research.** An active program at the intersection of auditor judgment and AI governance, with a
methodological paper in preparation and a dissertation designed to produce a measurable finding
rather than a conceptual argument. The work is positioned where behavioural accounting, information
systems, and emerging regulatory interest converge — a space where practitioner access is scarce and
I have it.

**Teaching.** I can teach auditing, internal controls, accounting information systems, enterprise
risk management, and behavioural accounting from fifteen years of doing the work, and I can teach
AI governance from having built and deployed a retrieval-augmented assistant inside a bank's audit
function rather than from reading about one. Having also examined banks as a regulator, I can put
students on both sides of the table — the firm's and the supervisor's — which in my experience is
where the concepts finally land.

**Practitioner access.** Recruiting professional respondents is the binding constraint on
behavioural accounting research, as my own study demonstrated at some cost. I bring fifteen years of
relationships across two global banks, a regulator, and the professional bodies. That is worth more
to a research group than another person competing for the same panel participants.

**External engagement.** Regulatory bodies are actively consulting on AI in audit. A department
positioned to contribute to that conversation — comment letters, working groups, executive education
— has visibility that is difficult to buy, and I know how to write for that audience because I have
sat on the receiving end of it.

## What I would want from a department

Colleagues who will push on the third link of my argument, because it is the one that needs
pushing. Institutional support for practitioner recruitment, which is the constraint that no
individual researcher solves alone. And students who will be working in AI-assisted audit functions
within five years, whether or not their curriculum has caught up.
