I teach the way I audit: state the finding first, show the evidence that produced it, and let the
room argue with the evidence rather than with me.

## Why a practitioner teaches this way

Fifteen years in audit and risk at Citigroup and JPMorgan Chase taught me that a workpaper nobody
can follow is not a finding, it is an opinion with a signature attached. The same discipline
applies in a classroom. A student who can recite a control framework but cannot walk me through
why a specific control failed has learned vocabulary, not judgment, and judgment is the thing I
was actually trained — and paid — to produce.

Two experiences shape how I run a room more than any pedagogy course would. At Citi, I designed
and deployed a retrieval-augmented AI review assistant that cut review cycle time by roughly 35%,
and I authored the governance proposal that went with it, built around the NIST AI Risk Management
Framework and SR 11-7. That project is the reason I do not teach AI in business courses as a tools
seminar. I have shipped a production system into a live audit function, watched where it helped,
and watched where it quietly shifted judgment onto the machine without anyone deciding that should
happen. Before the banks, I was a bank examiner with the Florida Office of Financial Regulation,
conducting safety-and-soundness examinations of state-chartered banks jointly and on alternating
cycles with the FDIC and the Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta. That work put me on the other side of
the table from every audit I have run since. I can teach a control test the way the firm performs
it and the way the examiner tears it apart, in the same session, because I have sat in both chairs.

## What I would teach, and how

**Auditing.** I would build a control-testing exercise from an anonymized scenario drawn from my
own thematic audit work — the kind of engagement I reported to a Board Audit Committee — rather
than a textbook vignette. Students test the control the way I tested it, then compare their
conclusion to the actual finding and defend the gap.

**Internal Controls and SOX.** Same method, narrower lens: a walkthrough built from a real control
narrative, stripped of identifying detail, where students have to locate the control gap before I
tell them where the auditor found it.

**Accounting Information Systems.** I would use the retrieval-augmented review assistant I built
at Citi as the running example — not the code, the design decisions: what the system was allowed
to retrieve, what it was not allowed to conclude, and why that boundary was drawn where it was.

**Enterprise Risk Management.** I would teach the Dodd-Frank resolution-plan process from the
inside, having coordinated fifty-plus stakeholders across annual submissions to the Federal
Reserve — students build a simplified resolution plan for a hypothetical institution and defend it
to a mock regulator panel.

**Behavioral Accounting.** This is where my dissertation research becomes the syllabus, not just
the professor's biography — see below.

**AI Governance.** Students draft a governance memo for a named AI use case using the same
structure I used at Citi: intended use, what the system may not decide alone, and the human
checkpoint that catches it when the system is wrong.

## What the doctoral program is teaching me about teaching

I am inside GEB7911, Qualitative Research Methods, this term, building and defending a
case-study research design from the ground up — problem statement, bounding the case, data
collection strategy, validation criteria — under a syllabus that treats anything below rigorous as
sub-level work. That is not background; it is direct preparation for the classroom. A student who
has only been shown finished case studies thinks a case study is a narrative. A student who has
had to defend why a case is bounded the way it is, and why that boundary threatens validity,
understands that a case study is an argument. I intend to teach methodology the second way, because
I am currently being examined the second way.

## My own research as a classroom example

My qualifying study set out to measure anchoring bias in long-term audit engagements against a
fully specified eleven-construct model. It did not fail; it hit a wall worth teaching from.
Applying my eligibility criteria to a commercial research panel of 334,976 participants returned
approximately twenty eligible people — a prevalence near six per hundred thousand, visible on the
platform's own configuration screen before I spent a dollar. I use this in class exactly as I
experienced it: as proof that "the panel has 300,000 people" and "I can study this population" are
two different claims, and that checking which one is true costs nothing and happens before the
budget is committed. Students design a screener for a specialist population of their choosing and
estimate its eligible prevalence before we discuss a single sampling method. Most are surprised by
their own number, which is the point.

## AI in the classroom

I do not tell students to embrace AI tools or to avoid them; both are abdications of the actual
question, which is where in a piece of work human judgment is allowed to be replaced and where it
is not. My research concerns automation bias directly — the tendency to defer to a system's output
even when a person's independent judgment would have caught the error — so I am not arguing this
from a general instinct about technology, I am arguing it from what the literature and my own
fieldwork show happens when review work is anchored to a machine-generated starting point. In
GEB7911 this term, Dr. Cristina Gonzalez's syllabus caps AI contribution to any assignment at 25%
and requires students to disclose what was AI-written versus self-written. I would set the same
kind of policy as an instructor, for the same reason she does: a hard cap forces a student to do
the reasoning themselves before the tool touches the page, and a disclosure requirement makes the
line visible instead of assumed. A policy that simply forbids the tools teaches nothing about how
to use them responsibly later, and a policy with no boundary at all teaches a student to stop
noticing where their own judgment ended.

## What I want from students

I want students who will tell me where my argument is wrong, in class, before the exam does it for
them.
