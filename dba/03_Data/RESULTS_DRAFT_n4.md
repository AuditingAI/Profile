**Honest interpretation — do not oversell this table.** At n=4, Cronbach's alpha is not a meaningful reliability estimate: two values are negative (RA, SAP), which is mathematically possible only when items violate the model's assumptions or — far more likely here — noise dominates at a sample this small. Even the "good-looking" alphas (TA=0.85, FR=0.83) should not be read as evidence of good measurement; with df this low, alpha is essentially uninformative in either direction. **Report the table for transparency, but explicitly state reliability could not be meaningfully assessed at the achieved sample size.** All means cluster narrowly around 3.5–4.2 (mild agreement), which is a substantive floor/ceiling observation worth one sentence, not a finding to lean on statistically.

## Qualitative Synthesis — Open-Text Responses (all 4 respondents answered)

All four respondents, independently, described the same underlying pattern without prompting toward it:

1. **An initial reference point (prior-year balance, budget, or management estimate) forms the starting expectation.** ("prior year balances are typically set as our expectation," "initial figure gives me a baseline," "the prior-year expense balance initially influenced my expectation.")
2. **All four describe actively testing and, when warranted, departing from that anchor** — none reported simply accepting it. Triggers for adjustment varied: regulatory/system changes (R1), evidence "completely off" from expectation (R2), reviewing supporting documentation and business changes (R3), identifying one-time/outlier items like an ERC credit (R4).
3. **This is a textbook anchoring-and-adjustment pattern** (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974): an anchor forms, and adjustment away from it occurs, but only in response to a deliberate evidentiary trigger — implying that in the absence of such a trigger, the anchor likely persists un-adjusted. That absence-of-trigger case is exactly the risk the dissertation's research question is about, and these four accounts illustrate the mechanism concretely, even though n is too small to test it statistically.

This qualitative pattern is arguably the most defensible finding at this sample size — it comes directly from practitioners' own words, requires no inferential statistics, and directly supports the theoretical model motivating the study.
