**When the Tool Agrees, Effort Stops**

This week's readings explain why people start effort and keep it going. My question is the opposite one: when
does effort quietly stop?

At Citi Internal Audit I built an AI tool to help review audit work. One day it reached the same conclusion I
already held, and I moved on. Later I asked myself whether I had checked the work or had simply felt confirmed.
Nothing went wrong that day, but the moment stayed with me, because none of my training told me to keep
digging once the evidence and the tool agreed.

Read together, the three papers suggest why that moment is risky.

Locke and Latham (2002) show that specific, difficult goals raise performance, but they also warn that
performance goals can hurt complex tasks, where learning goals work better. Audit targets are mostly
performance goals: files closed, findings cleared, deadlines met. Judgement is the complex task those goals
can crowd out.

Deci, Olafsen and Ryan (2017) distinguish autonomous from controlled motivation. Deadlines and metrics push
controlled motivation, which narrows effort to what is measured. Re-checking an answer that already agrees
with you is rarely measured.

Grant et al. (2007) show that contact with the people your work helps sustains persistence. Auditors almost
never meet the depositors or investors a missed error would harm, and the letter condition in their first
experiment suggests that information about beneficiaries is weaker than contact.

Last week Dr. Gajendran asked when AI is a job resource and when it is a demand. I would add a third
possibility: it can quietly lower the effort we spend on the part of the job that matters most, because
agreement feels like evidence. Keisha's point about what happens to time saved by automation fits here.
The time saved may not go to deeper judgement at all.

A study I would like to see: compare reviewers given a performance goal with reviewers given a learning goal,
with and without a visible reminder of who a missed error affects, and measure how long they keep checking
after an AI tool agrees with their initial view. That is my proposed extension, not something these papers
test.

My question: if goals, controlled motivation and distant beneficiaries all reduce persistence, what would
keep a professional sceptical at the exact moment a trusted tool agrees with them?
