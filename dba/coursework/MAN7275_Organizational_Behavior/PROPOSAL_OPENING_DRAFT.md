## Draft opening (about 230 words)

In the autumn of 2026, artificial intelligence systems produced claimed proofs of results that mathematicians had
pursued for decades, including a partial result on the Navier–Stokes equations and, reportedly, a proof of the
Riemann hypothesis [CHECK: Scientific American, 13 Oct and 20 Nov 2026]. What followed is instructive for any
organization that relies on human review of AI output. The announcements arrived before the verification and set
the public frame. A single sign error, once accepted, carried into papers that built on it and forced withdrawals
[CHECK: Retraction Watch, 8 Oct 2026]. And leading mathematicians asked for machine-checkable proofs before
accepting the results, because expert reading alone was not trusted to catch what the system got wrong [CHECK:
Quanta Magazine, 19 Oct 2026].

Mathematics has such a checker. Audit review does not. When an AI tool reviews an engagement and agrees with the
reviewer's initial view, the remaining safeguard is the reviewer's own willingness to challenge the work, under
workloads and reward systems that rarely make challenge the priority (Sherf, Venkataramani & Gajendran, 2019).
This proposal asks whether AI agreement reduces reviewers' challenge of the work, whether that effect is
stronger under high workload, and whether rewards for challenge weaken it. Drawing on anchoring and
confirmation research and on multiple-task pursuit theory, it develops a moderated model linking AI agreement
to reduced challenge and, through it, to judgments that converge on the AI rather than on the evidence.
