### Work group diversity review (van Knippenberg & Schippers, 2007)
What it is:      a review of why diversity sometimes helps teams and sometimes hurts them.
Why it works:    two forces at once: social categorization (us vs them, weaker cooperation) and
                 information/decision-making (more perspectives, better decisions).
When it fails:   main effects are inconsistent; the answer depends on moderators (task, time, identity).
Advances mine:   an AI that agrees with the reviewer removes the "different perspective" a second person adds,
                 so the information benefit of a diverse review team shrinks.
Say:             "The review says diversity is not good or bad on its own; the moderators decide, which is why
                 main-effect studies keep disagreeing."
Ask:             "If both forces always operate, should a study measure both mechanisms, not just the outcome?"

### Separation, variety, disparity (Harrison & Klein, 2007)
What it is:      "diversity" is three different constructs: separation (positions on one scale), variety
                 (different kinds of knowledge), disparity (unequal share of status or pay).
Why it works:    each comes from a different theory and predicts a different outcome, so each needs its own
                 measure (for example, Blau's index for variety).
When it fails:   when a study measures one type but theorizes another; the result cannot be interpreted.
Advances mine:   the cleanest fit for my chain: AI agreement collapses separation (everyone moves to the AI's
                 view), cuts variety, and adds disparity (the tool's answer carries more weight than a junior's).
Say:             "Convergence on an AI is a loss of separation, and the authors would say measure it as
                 separation, not as variety."
Ask:             "Could an AI tool count as a member that creates disparity, because its output is treated as
                 higher status?"

### Surface- and deep-level diversity over time (Harrison et al., 2002)
What it is:      surface-level differences (age, gender) matter early; deep-level differences (values,
                 attitudes) matter more as people work together.
Why it works:    time and collaboration replace first impressions with real knowledge of each other.
When it fails:   in short-lived teams, deep-level differences never surface.
Advances mine:   recurring audit engagements are long-tenure teams, so deep-level agreement (with each other and
                 with the tool) should matter more each year, which fits the convergence link.
Say:             "Long engagements are where deep-level similarity takes over, and that is where drift
                 toward one view should be strongest."
Ask:             "Does this mean diversity studies with one-time surveys mostly capture surface-level effects?"

### Faultlines (Bezrukova et al., 2009; idea from Lau & Murnighan, 1998)
What it is:      hypothetical lines that split a team into subgroups when several attributes line up.
Why it works:    aligned differences make subgroups visible and invite us-versus-them.
When it fails:   strong identification with the whole team can blunt the harm.
Advances mine:   a team can split into "trusts the AI" and "checks the AI"; strong team identity around
                 professional skepticism could stop that split from turning into deference.
Say:             "A faultline can form around a tool: the people who rely on it and the people who check it."
Ask:             "Do faultlines based on information (training, function) behave differently from those based on
                 demographics?"

### Links across readings
- All four say diversity has no single effect; theory must say which kind and which mechanism.
- Harrison & Klein give the measurement rule the others need: name the construct, then pick the index.
- Time (Harrison et al.) and identity (Bezrukova et al.) are the moderators that keep coming back.
