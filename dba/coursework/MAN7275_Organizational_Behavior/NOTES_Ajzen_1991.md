**Big idea.** General attitudes and traits predict specific behaviors poorly (pp. 180–181). Behavior-specific
constructs do better: attitude, subjective norm and perceived behavioral control (PBC) predict intention, and
intention plus PBC predict behavior (p. 179). TPB is reasoned action plus PBC (pp. 181, 183).

**Theory and mechanism**

| Construct | Definition | p. |
|---|---|---|
| Intention | Motivation; "how hard people are willing to try" | 181 |
| Attitude | Favorable or unfavorable evaluation of performing the behavior | 188 |
| Subjective norm | Perceived social pressure to perform or not | 188 |
| PBC | Perceived ease or difficulty; akin to Bandura's self-efficacy, not Rotter's locus of control | 183–184 |
| Beliefs | Behavioral, normative and control beliefs underlie each | 189 |

PBC predicts behavior directly: confidence raises persistence at equal intention, and realistic PBC proxies
actual control (pp. 184–185). Prediction needs compatible measures, stable intentions and accurate PBC (p. 185).

**Method and scope.** Narrative review of recent TPB studies in summary tables, with reanalysed data sets (pp. 179, 187, 189).

**Key findings**
1. Behavior from intention plus PBC: R from .20 to .78, average .51; weakest for losing weight and getting an
   "A", the least volitional (p. 187).
2. Intention from the three predictors: 16 studies, R from .43 to .94, average .71; PBC significant in every
   study, attitude in all but one, subjective norm mixed (p. 189).
3. The intuitive intention x control interaction appeared once in seven tests, marginally (p. 188).
4. Skim section: belief-based measures explain only 10 to 36% of global attitude (p. 195); moral obligation adds
   3 to 6% (p. 200); past behavior keeps a residual effect, so sufficiency is open (pp. 202–204).

**Contributions.** A parsimonious model of *when* attitudes predict action; adds control; belief-level
intervention targets (p. 206).

**Critique angles**
- Mostly self-report within one questionnaire; shared-method variance inflates R, as Ajzen concedes for past
  behavior (p. 202).
- Subjective norm is the weak link (p. 189), yet in inspected professions social pressure should be central;
  the global measure may be the problem.
- A deliberative model: Ajzen raises automatic versus reasoned responding (p. 197). Anchoring operates below
  intention, so TPB may explain whether someone plans to check, not whether the check works.

**Connections**
- *Person-situation debate:* starts from the same Mischel (1968) problem as Schneider (p. 180) but moves to
  behavior-specific constructs instead of traits. Situation strength (Judge and Zapata) maps loosely onto volitional control.
- *Harrison et al. (2006):* borrow the compatibility principle and broaden the behavior instead of narrowing the attitude.
- *Sutton and Staw:* Figure 1 (p. 182) is a diagram whose why the text supplies.
- *Yasir's research:* intention to scrutinize an AI output that agrees with a prior view is a clean TPB target:
  attitude (is re-checking worthwhile), norm (does the manager or inspector expect it), PBC (time budget, access
  to the model's reasoning). PBC is least realistic when "new and unfamiliar elements" appear (p. 185), as with new AI tools.

**Discussion seeds**
1. If PBC predicts poorly when situations are new (p. 185), is TPB the wrong tool for new technology, or is
   miscalibrated PBC itself the finding?
2. Norms were the weakest predictor (p. 189). Would they stay weak in regulated professions where behavior is
   inspected, and what would that say about TPB's generality?
