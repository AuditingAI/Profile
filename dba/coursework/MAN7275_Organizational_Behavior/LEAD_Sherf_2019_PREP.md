## Slide 1 · Question and contribution
- **Puzzle:** managers say fairness matters, yet act unfairly. Why?
- **Move:** from the "actor-centric" view (the manager's traits) to a **context-centric** view: workload and
  rewards. Authors: putting the onus for fairness entirely on managers "might, ironically, be unfair."
- **Second contribution:** justice is treated as **one task competing with others** for time and effort,
  not an isolated behaviour.

## Slide 2 · Theory and model (spend time here)
- **Theory:** multiple-task pursuit framework (Neal et al., 2017; self-regulation). Under scarce resources,
  people allocate effort by perceived importance and incentives.
- **Why technical tasks win:** clear metrics, more downward feedback, Kerr (1975) "rewarding A while hoping for B".
- **Hypotheses (p. 475):**
  - **H1:** workload → (+) prioritisation of technical tasks → (−) justice rule adherence (negative indirect effect).
  - **H2:** rewards for justice moderate workload → prioritisation (weaker when rewards are high).
  - **H3:** moderated mediation: the indirect effect is less negative when justice rewards are high.
- Justice rule adherence = all four rules (distributive, procedural, interpersonal, informational), modelled as
  one higher-order construct.
- A **pilot study** (Appendix A) validated two assumptions (managers see technical tasks as more central; firms
  reward them more) and built the reward and prioritisation scales.

```
Workload ──(+)──► Prioritisation of technical tasks ──(−)──► Justice rule adherence
                         ▲
              Rewards for justice (weakens the first arrow)
```

## Slide 3 · Method (three designs, each covering the others' weakness)
| | Study 1 | Study 2 | Study 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Design | Experience sampling, 2 weeks, two surveys a day | Cross-sectional, multisource field survey | Lab simulation, 2 × 2 experiment |
| Sample | 107 U.S. managers (MTurk), 862 day-level observations | 166 manager–employee triads, India (market research firm) | 239 undergraduates |
| Workload | Daily self-report | Self-report, last 3 months | **Manipulated:** 20 vs 30 minutes for the same two tasks |
| Justice rewards | Perceived (self-report) | Perceived (self-report) | **Manipulated:** scorecard 70/20/10 vs 45/45/10 |
| Justice adherence | **Manager self-report** (11 items) | **Employee-rated** (short Colquitt 2001, 3 items per rule) | **Coded** by blind raters (message to the employee not promoted) |
| Technical performance | Not measured | Rated by a second employee | Coded (client-acquisition proposal) |

## Slide 4 · Key findings
- **Study 1:** workload → prioritisation (γ = .13); interaction with rewards (γ = −.04, p = .01). With low rewards
  the slope is .21; with high rewards .05, n.s. Prioritisation → justice (γ = −.16). Indirect effect significant
  at low rewards, not at high.
- **Study 2:** same pattern (workload → prioritisation B = .19; interaction B = −.09, p = .03; prioritisation →
  justice B = −.18). Prioritisation was **not** related to technical performance (B = −.04, n.s.).
- **Study 3:** interaction on prioritisation (F = 4.14, p = .04, η²p = .02). Justice performance was lowest in
  high workload + low justice rewards. Rewards did **not** change technical performance under high workload.
- **Headline:** under high workload, rewarding justice raised fair treatment **without a measurable cost** to
  technical performance (Studies 2 and 3). The authors call this a hint that the trade-off "is not always zero-sum".

## Slide 5 · Authors' discussion
- **Implications:** manage managers' workload (job design, flexibility; they cite Gajendran et al., 2015);
  put justice into formal evaluation and rewards (360-degree feedback); **pre-commit** fixed time to justice
  tasks (meetings, town halls, "management by walking around").
- They do **not** argue justice should always outrank technical tasks; context decides.
- **Their stated limits:** Study 1 measured prioritisation and justice at the same time; Study 2 is
  cross-sectional; Study 3's students and simulation limit generalisability; technical performance was never
  rated by top management; justice treated as one construct.

## Slide 6 · Your critique and future research (your strongest slide; prompts only)
1. **Who measured justice?** Study 1 is all manager self-report. In Study 2 the managers **nominated** the
   employees who rated them. In Study 3, prioritisation was self-reported after the simulation. This ties to your
   5 Nov class point: ratings as weak evidence.
2. **How robust is the moderator?** In Study 2 the workload × rewards interaction was **not significant without
   control variables** (B = −.04, p = .44). The authors report this openly in the Study 2 results. Ask what that means for H2.
3. **Effect sizes are small** (Study 3 interaction η²p = .02). Small but meaningful daily effects, or fragile?
4. **The facets differ.** In Study 2 (footnote 7) the indirect effect held for distributive and interpersonal justice,
   was marginal for procedural, and was **not significant for informational** justice (explaining decisions). Yet
   Study 3's justice task was mostly an explanation. Is "explaining" protected, or measured differently?
5. **Your extension (raw material, your words):** what happens when a tool takes part of the technical task?
   In audit review, the manager's challenge works like a justice task: effortful, poorly metered, easily cut under
   load. An AI summary that agrees makes skipping it cheaper. Rewards for challenge would be the moderator, exactly
   as rewards for justice are here. One study: vary reviewer workload and whether challenge is rewarded; measure
   whether reviewers accept an AI conclusion without testing it.

## Slide 7 · Connections and two questions
- **Judge & Piccolo (2004):** Sherf's moderator is a contingent reward. What is rewarded decides what busy managers keep.
- **Lin, Scott & Matta (2019):** good leader behaviour costs resources; under load it is what goes.
- **Colquitt & Zipay (2015):** justice (rule adherence) vs fairness (overall judgment). Sherf measures the rules.
- **Avery et al. (2023):** if justice rewards are uneven across units, the workload effect could fall unevenly on employees.
- **Dr. Buckman (5 Nov):** contribution comes from moderators that change a known effect. Rewards for justice do that.
- Draft questions (pick two, rewrite):
  - "If rewarding justice did not cost technical performance, why don't firms do it? What does that say about how they measure managers?"
  - "Which of your own work tasks would be cut first in a 20-minute version of your week?"
