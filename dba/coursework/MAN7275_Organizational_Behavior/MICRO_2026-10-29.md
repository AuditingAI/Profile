### Mitchell & Daniels (2003): motivation, the map
What it is:      a handbook review of the main motivation theories at work.
Why it works:    motivation = the forces that set **direction, intensity and persistence** of effort.
When it fails:   theories explain choices to try; ability and situation still limit results.
Advances mine:   gives the vocabulary: AI agreement may cut *persistence* in checking, not ability.
Say:             "Most theories explain why people start effort; my question is why reviewers *stop* checking."
Ask:             "Which theory best explains when effort stops, not when it starts?"

### Deci, Olafsen & Ryan (2017): self-determination theory
What it is:      people thrive when three needs are met: **autonomy, competence, relatedness**.
Why it works:    autonomous motivation (I value it) beats controlled motivation (pressure, contingent pay).
When it fails:   in cultures or jobs where control is expected; boring tasks need a reason, not just choice.
Advances mine:   AI can raise competence but cut autonomy if the reviewer only checks the machine. Controlled
                 motivation (deadlines, metrics) may push "accept the AI answer".
Say:             "Whether AI is a resource or a demand may depend on whether it supports or replaces autonomy."
Ask:             "Can a strong control environment like audit still produce autonomous motivation?"

### Locke & Latham (2002): goal setting (Nicole leads)
What it is:      **specific, difficult goals** beat "do your best".
Why it works:    goals direct attention, raise effort, extend persistence, and push strategy search.
When it fails:   complex tasks (use learning goals), no commitment, no feedback, low ability, conflicting goals.
Advances mine:   hard performance targets in audit (close findings fast) may reward quick acceptance of AI
                 output over judgement. Complexity is their own boundary condition.
Say:             "For complex judgement work, their own paper says learning goals, not hard numbers."
Ask (Nicole):    "Where does goal setting conflict with self-determination theory, assigned goals vs autonomy?"

### Grant et al. (2007): impact and motivation maintenance (Ana leads)
What it is:      brief **contact with the people your work helps** raises perceived impact and persistence.
Why it works:    seeing impact makes the task significant (Humphrey's task significance, made visible).
Evidence:        fundraisers who met a scholarship student later spent more time on calls and raised more
                 money [CHECK: ~142% more phone time, ~171% more money, one month later].
When it fails:   if contact is staged, rare, or the beneficiary is distant from the worker's control.
Advances mine:   auditors rarely meet the people audit protects (depositors, investors). Low perceived impact
                 may make an agreeing AI answer easier to accept.
Say:             "Would auditors stay sceptical longer if they saw who a missed error hurts?"
Ask (Ana):       "Is the effect contact itself, or the story of impact? Could a video do the same?"

### Links across readings
- Goal setting vs SDT: assigned goals work when people **accept** them (commitment) and feel ownership.
- Grant extends Humphrey (Week 2): task significance works because impact is **seen**.
- SDT ties to Week 2: autonomy (Gajendran), resources (JD-R) are the same needs.
