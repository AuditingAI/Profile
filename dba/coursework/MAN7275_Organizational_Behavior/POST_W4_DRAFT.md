**When the Leader Is Tired, the Tool Becomes the Reviewer**

This week's readings ask what effective leadership looks like. My question is narrower: what happens to a
leader's review of the team's work when the leader runs out of energy and an AI tool is willing to do the
reading?

Piccolo and Colquitt (2006) show that transformational leaders shape how employees experience their jobs,
including whether the work feels meaningful and whether they get useful feedback. In audit, one of the most
valuable forms of that feedback is the manager's challenge: "How do you know? What else could explain this?"
That is intellectual stimulation in practice.

Lin, Scott and Matta (2019) suggest this behaviour has a cost. Days with more transformational behaviour than
usual were linked to more emotional exhaustion for the leaders themselves. Alexander's post shows how success can
add to that load. I would add a second risk. When a reviewing manager is depleted, the easiest move is to let an
AI summary stand in for their own reading. If the tool agrees with the preparer, the review becomes a signature
rather than a challenge.

Judge and Piccolo (2004) help explain why this could become the norm. Contingent reward, meaning clear
expectations linked to rewards, matters alongside transformational leadership. If the expectation rewarded is
files closed on time, a tired manager who accepts the tool's agreement is meeting the goal. The quiet failure is
that the team learns that agreement, not challenge, is what the leader values.

I saw a version of this at Citi Internal Audit, where I built an AI tool to support review. Its value depended
on whether managers used it to raise questions or to avoid them.

A study I would like to see: track reviewing managers' daily exhaustion alongside how often their review notes
challenge a conclusion that an AI tool has already supported. My proposed extension is that exhaustion predicts
more acceptance of AI-supported conclusions. The readings do not test this.

My question: if intellectual stimulation is both the most valuable and the most draining part of leadership,
should organizations protect it, for example by limiting how many reviews a manager signs, rather than expect
leaders to supply it on demand?
