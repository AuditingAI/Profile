# Note card · Smith et al. (2018), Upsides to dark and downsides to bright · MAN 7275 Week 1

Smith, M. B., Hill, A. D., Wallace, J. C., Recendes, T., & Judge, T. A. (2018). Upsides to dark and
downsides to bright personality: A multidomain review and future research agenda. *Journal of
Management, 44*(1), 191–217. https://doi.org/10.1177/0149206317733511
Read in full by Claude 14 Oct 2026 for Yasir's prep (skim-level card); Yasir to read himself.

**Big idea.** "Bright = good, dark = bad" is an oversimplification. Socially undesirable traits
(Dark Triad, hubris) can help in some contexts, and socially desirable traits (Big Five, HEXACO) can
hurt at extreme levels or in the wrong context (pp. 192–193).

**Theory and mechanism.** No single theory. Bright and dark are defined by social desirability, not
morality (p. 192). Mechanisms are two kinds of moderation:
- *Context*: e.g., Machiavellians show challenging citizenship behavior under transformational leaders
  (p. 195).
- *Nonlinearity* ("too much of a good thing"): an inverted U between conscientiousness and well-being;
  conscientiousness helps more in complex jobs (p. 196).
They point to trait activation and socioanalytic ("get along vs. get ahead") theory as the lenses
(pp. 207–209).

**Scope.** Narrative multidomain review: OB, leadership, HR, strategy, entrepreneurship, teams.
Database search terms plus management and personality journals and reviewer input (p. 193). Table 1
lists about 30 traits (pp. 194–195).

**Key findings (as reviewed).**
1. Narcissism relates to leader emergence but not effectiveness (Grijalva et al. meta-analysis;
   p. 197). Narcissists are rated positively in interviews and assessment centers (p. 198).
2. CEO narcissism relates to *variance* in firm performance, not its level (p. 199).
3. Bright downsides: dependable team members hurt adaptation when change is needed (LePine; p. 196).
   People can be too agreeable to dissent (p. 196). Agreeable raters distort performance evaluations
   (p. 198). Agreeable directors may fail to monitor a CEO (p. 208).
4. Teams are the biggest gap; all-agreeable groups risk groupthink (pp. 201–202).

**Contributions.** Brings scattered "upside/downside" findings across domains into one agenda.
Measurement guidance: short dark scales such as the 12-item Dirty Dozen raise content-validity
concerns (p. 202). Ideal-point and "too little/too much" scaling can detect curvilinearity
(pp. 203–204). Calls to define constructs before validating them, given redundancy (p. 204).

**Critique angles (mine).**
- If every trait has upsides and downsides depending on context and level, the claim is unfalsifiable
  unless the contexts are specified ahead of time. Few are.
- Table 1 is a "list of variables" in Sutton & Staw's sense. The review catalogs more than it
  explains, and many cited findings are single studies or "in press."
- Anecdote creeps in (Eric Schmidt as "too nice," p. 200). The label "dark" carries the evaluative
  load the authors say it does not.

**Connections.**
- *Sutton & Staw*: useful foil. The agenda asks for mechanisms (p. 206), but the body is mostly
  findings.
- *Judge & Zapata*: same idea of moderation, here extended to sign reversals (the trait helps in one
  context and hurts in another), not just stronger or weaker effects. Their reversed attention-to-detail
  result is a bright-trait downside.
- *Davis-Blake & Pfeffer*: the "dark" framing invites the individual-blame attribution they warned
  about (scandals blamed on dark leaders, p. 208).
- *His research*: real link through agreeableness. An AI that agrees with the auditor works like an
  overly agreeable teammate that will not dissent (pp. 196, 202). Skepticism-related traits may be a
  "bright" trait with a curvilinear effect on review quality.

**Discussion-question seeds.**
1. Is "too much of a good thing" a theory or a description? What would falsify it for
   conscientiousness in audit work?
2. If agreeable directors under-monitor (p. 208), does an agreeable AI assistant create the same
   governance gap for auditors?
