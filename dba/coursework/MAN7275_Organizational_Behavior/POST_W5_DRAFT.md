**Too busy to challenge: what Sherf et al. imply for review work**

Colquitt and Zipay (2015) separate justice, meaning adherence to specific rules such as consistency, respect and
explanation, from fairness, an employee's overall judgment. Sherf, Venkataramani and Gajendran (2019) ask why
managers break those rules even when they value them. Their answer moves the cause from the manager to the job.
Under high workload, managers put technical tasks first because technical work has clear metrics and gets
rewarded, while fair treatment rarely does. When the organisation rewards fairness, the effect weakens. In
Studies 2 and 3, rewarding fairness raised fair treatment without a measurable cost to technical performance.

What interests me is the measurement. In Study 1, managers rated their own fairness each day. In Study 2,
managers chose the employees who rated them. In Study 3, prioritising was self-reported after the simulation.
The design is strong because the three studies cover each other's weaknesses, but each still leans on a rating
someone had reason to give. That matters because the moderator is the paper's main contribution, and in
Study 2 the workload × rewards interaction was not significant without the control variables.

My own field adds a twist. In audit review, the manager's challenge ("How do you know? What else could
explain this?") behaves like a justice task: effortful, rarely metered, and the first thing cut when files are
due. Now add an AI tool that summarises the work and agrees with the preparer. The technical task gets
cheaper, but so does skipping the challenge. Sherf et al. predict that under load, the reviewer will accept
the agreement and move on, unless challenge itself is rewarded. That is a testable boundary condition: does a
tool that lowers the cost of the technical task free time for the relational one, or does it make
agreement the default?

**My question for the class:** If rewarding fair treatment costs nothing in performance, as Studies 2 and 3
suggest, why do so few organisations put it in managers' scorecards? Is the barrier measurement, or the
belief that fairness is "nice to have"?

**References**

rewards on managers' justice rule adherence. *Academy of Management Journal, 62*(2), 469–502.

## Reply to Alexander Ponce (about 130 words)

Alexander, your question about whether we reward fairness ratings or fair decisions fits Sherf et al. (2019)
closely. Their fairness measures came from different sources in each study: managers rated themselves in Study
1, and in Study 2 managers nominated the employees who rated them. If ratings can rise faster than decisions
improve, as you suggest, then a reward system built on those ratings could teach managers to manage the rating.
Campbell's law (1979) warns about exactly this: the more a measure drives decisions, the more it is distorted.
Would your decision simulation also track whether participants could tell a well-explained bad decision from a
plainly explained good one? That might separate trust in the speaker from judgment of the process.
