# Note card · Schneider (1987), The people make the place · MAN 7275 Week 1

Schneider, B. (1987). The people make the place. *Personnel Psychology, 40*(3), 437–453. SIOP
Presidential Address (1985), lightly revised. Read in full by Claude 14 Oct 2026 for Yasir's prep; Yasir to
read himself. Classmate Atemlefac leads.

**Big idea.** The attributes of people, not structure, technology or environment, are "the fundamental
determinants of organizational behavior" (p. 437). Lewin's B = f(P, E) becomes E = f(P, B) (p. 438).
Organizations only *look* like they cause behavior because we study them after people have sorted themselves (p. 440).

**Theory and mechanism: the ASA cycle**

| Step | What happens | p. |
|---|---|---|
| Attraction | People are drawn to careers and firms matching their interests and personality (Holland) | 441 |
| Selection | Firms hire for competencies but choose people sharing many personal attributes | 444 |
| Attrition | Misfits leave, so stayers are more alike than the applicant pool | 442 |
| Goals at the hub | Founder's goals shape the structures that attract people (Prop. 6, Figure 1) | 443–445 |

Result: organizations "severely restricted the range of types of people" (Prop. 7, p. 444). Restricted range
makes compositional similarity look situational (Prop. 5, p. 443), the artefact Bowers found in lab
experiments (pp. 439–440).

**Method and scope.** Conceptual essay, seven propositions, no new data; borrows from interactional and
vocational psychology, biodata and turnover research (pp. 438–442).

**Key claims**
1. Homogeneity is a survival risk: an ingrown firm fits a narrow niche and fails when the environment shifts,
   "dry rot" (pp. 445–446). Changing structure without changing people will not work (p. 446).
2. Personality measures are a yardstick, not a micrometer: weak within one job, useful for typing whole
   organizations (p. 447). Recruitment, not selection, is the lever for widening the pool (p. 448).
3. **Job attitudes:** people in a setting share attitudes because they are similar people in similar
   conditions, not because of social construction (p. 449). The same pay can satisfy mental health workers and
   dissatisfy stockbrokers; attitudes "may come with them to a setting" (p. 450).

**Contributions.** Moves person effects to the organizational level; a causal story for homogeneity,
culture (p. 448) and inertia.

**Critique angles**
- The full cycle is asserted, not shown; selection on shared attributes is hypothesized (p. 444). Strong *why*,
  borrowed data.
- Overcorrects: "Enough is enough" (p. 451) swaps situationism for personism, losing the interactionist premise
  he starts from (p. 439).
- Founder-centric goals (p. 443) fit young firms better than mature, regulated ones whose structure is partly
  set by law and standards.

**Connections**
- *Person-situation debate:* the strong person-side position. Davis-Blake and Pfeffer (1989) call dispositional
  claims a mirage; Barrick and Mount (2005) defend personality; Judge and Zapata (2015) reconcile via situation
  strength. Irony: ASA itself manufactures restricted range, one reason trait effects look small.
- *Sutton and Staw:* a "why" story with no data, the trade they invite journals to accept.
- *Yasir's research:* ASA predicts homogeneous audit teams. If a team shares a prior view and the AI tool agrees,
  no dissenting "non-right type" may be left to challenge it. Within-firm restricted range could also mask
  individual differences in a single-firm sample.

**Discussion seeds**
1. Should audit firms deliberately recruit "non-right types" (p. 448) as an independence safeguard, and could
   AI tools trained on firm data deepen the homogeneity Schneider warns about?
2. Schneider says job attitudes are compositional, not situational (pp. 449–450). What design would separate
   the two, and which of today's readings supplies the tools?
