# Note card · Davis-Blake & Pfeffer (1989), Just a mirage · MAN 7275 Week 1

Davis-Blake, A., & Pfeffer, J. (1989). Just a mirage: The search for dispositional effects in
organizational research. *Academy of Management Review, 14*(3), 385–400.
Read in full by Claude 14 Oct 2026 for Yasir's prep; Yasir to read himself.

**Big idea.** The late-1980s revival of dispositional research (Staw & Ross, 1985; Staw et al., 1986;
Gerhart, 1987) is flawed conceptually and methodologically. Organizations are strong situations, so
"it is unlikely that dispositional effects are as important as situational effects" (p. 386).

**Theory and mechanism.** Situationist, with sociological roots (institutional theory, social
information processing).
- *Strong situations* (pp. 387–388): institutionalized roles, culture, pay, goals, job design and
  socialization constrain behavior, so traits have little room to show.
- *Adaptation* (pp. 388–389): dispositions are changed by work (Kohn & Schooler), so "stable trait"
  is doubtful. Genetic evidence covers intrinsic but not extrinsic or overall satisfaction (p. 389).
- *Against "the people make the place"* (Schneider): random assignment studies (Zimbardo), multiple
  memberships, and social influence show situations are more than their members (pp. 389–391).

**Method.** Conceptual critique; no new data. Targets longitudinal job-satisfaction studies.

**Key findings (arguments).**
1. Circularity: using intertemporal correlations both to infer a disposition and to show its effect
   is "circular reasoning" (pp. 391–392). Correlations only preserve rank order; mean levels can move
   a lot while ranks hold (p. 392).
2. Omitted stable causes (real earnings, race, gender, job conditions, social networks) produce
   autocorrelated error; reported effects may be "a mirage created by omitted variables and
   autocorrelated error" (p. 394). Occupation-level job measures are too crude (p. 394).
3. Staw et al. (1986) is credited as "a major advance" because the disposition was measured
   separately from the outcome (pp. 392–393).
4. Four fixes (pp. 395–396): define and measure dispositions separately; model nondispositional
   causes; test relative importance; theorize the sources of dispositions.

**Contributions.** Set the methodological bar the field later met (separate measurement, controls,
relative importance). Added a normative cost argument: dispositional selection may favor workers who
accept poor conditions and lets managers blame individuals, a fundamental attribution error (p. 397).

**Critique angles (mine).**
- Asymmetric burden. Dispositionalists must measure the trait; the claim that "most organizational
  settings are strong" (p. 387) is supported by citation, not by measuring situation strength.
  Judge & Zapata (2015) later did exactly that, and found personality matters most in weak jobs.
- The target is job *attitudes*, but the conclusion is about "dispositional research" in general.
  Performance validity, the ground Barrick & Mount (2005) fight on, is barely addressed.
- Their own adaptation evidence implies reciprocal person and situation causation, which undercuts the
  "which matters more" framing they ask others to test.

**Connections.**
- *Sutton & Staw*: a textbook "data are not theory" case. A stability correlation shows *that*;
  they demand the *why* (mechanisms of inertia, p. 388). Irony: Staw is a target here.
- *Barrick & Mount* name this paper as a classic critic (p. 359). *Judge & Zapata* resolve it
  through interactionism, though with occupation-level coding that this paper calls crude (p. 394).
- *Smith et al.*: the "dark/bright" labels assume traits are stable and context-free, the assumption
  attacked here.
- *His research*: same trap. If an auditor's acceptance of an AI output is treated as a trait
  (credulity, automation bias) inferred from the same acceptance data, it is circular. The design
  should manipulate the situation (AI agrees or not) and measure any trait separately. Audit
  methodology and review are a strong situation, which may mute individual differences.

**Discussion-question seeds.**
1. If organizations are strong situations, is a strong audit methodology a design choice to *make*
   personality irrelevant, and does AI agreement strengthen or weaken that situation?
2. Is the "fundamental attribution error" cost (p. 397) a scientific objection or a values argument,
   and does it belong in a theory paper?
