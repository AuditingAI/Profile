# PART ONE · International Business — GEB 7365

## 1 · The multinationality–performance relationship

**The question.** Do more international firms perform better? The field has answered *yes*, *no*,
*U-shaped* and *inverted-U*, all with data.

**The reconciling account — the three-stage / sigmoid theory**
**Contractor, Kundu & Hsu (2003)**, *JIBS* 34(1): 5–18 · **PDF pp. 2–4, 6, 8–11**

| Stage | Slope | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Negative | Liability of foreignness · initial learning costs · insufficient scale |
| 2 | Positive | Scale, scope and learning benefits outweigh the costs |
| 3 | Negative | Cost and complexity of managing widely scattered operations |

**Why the field disagreed, in their words** (PDF p. 4):
> *"The data may be capturing only part of an overall sigmoid (S-shaped) function… Depending on
> which part of Figure 1 we examined, we can find linear (positive and negative), U-shaped, and
> inverted-U-shaped segments."*

**The figures to look at:** Figure 1 (the curve itself), Figures 2–3 (capital-intensive vs
knowledge-based service sectors). **PDF pp. 8–11.**

**Sector finding.** Knowledge-based services — advertising, market research, securities, publishing
— stray into suboptimal Stage 3 more readily than capital-intensive ones (airlines, hotels,
construction, retail), because fixed-asset risk is lower. **PDF pp. 12–13.**

**Line worth quoting:** *"Few companies possess the managerial tools… that would tell them when they
have over-internationalized."* **PDF p. 13.**

## 2 · Liability of foreignness

**The idea.** A foreign firm bears costs a local one does not — unfamiliarity with language, law and
context, plus local preference for local firms.

**Where it is theorised**
Contractor **PDF pp. 4, 13, 15** · Zaheer & Mosakowski, cited at Contractor **PDF p. 4** and Mezias
**PDF pp. 2–3**

**Where it is *measured* — the important one**
**Mezias (2002)**, *SMJ* 23(3): 229–244 · **PDF pp. 2–4, 8, 11–12, 14–15**

The methodological move, in his words (**PDF p. 3**):
> *"While most performance measures aggregate advantages and disadvantages, labor lawsuits only
> measure labor-related disadvantage. Also, income shifting or transfer pricing between parent and
> subsidiary do not affect this measure."*

**Design:** 486 British, German and Japanese subsidiaries in the US, matched to 486 US-owned firms
in the **same industries and cities**. Then, within the foreign subsample, tests whether staffing
strategy, autonomy, number of affiliated US firms and subsidiary age change exposure. **PDF pp. 2–5.**

**Why it matters to your work.** This is the template: a construct everyone agreed existed but
nobody could isolate, made measurable by finding an observable trace that isolates *one* thing.
That is the move your own study needs.

## 3 · Distance — the CAGE framework

**Ghemawat (2001)**, *HBR* 79(8): 137–147 · **PDF pp. 5, 11** for the framework itself

**Four distances, not one:** **C**ultural · **A**dministrative or political · **G**eographic ·
**E**conomic. Administrative distance is discussed at **PDF pp. 4–8**; cultural at **pp. 5–6**.

**What it critiques: country portfolio analysis** — **PDF pp. 9–11**. CPA looks at GDP, consumer
wealth and propensity to consume:
> *"It ignores the costs and risks of doing business in a new market. Most of those costs and risks
> result from barriers created by distance."*

**The case:** Star TV. News Corporation paid **$825m** (1993–95); losses roughly **$500m** across
FY1996–99. Satellite delivery solved *geographic* distance — the one kind that was not the problem.
**PDF pp. 3–4.**

## 4 · The Uppsala internationalization model

**Johanson & Vahlne (1977).** Firms internationalise incrementally, entering psychically close
markets first and committing more as market knowledge accumulates.

**Not assigned directly — reached through the citing papers:**
Contractor **PDF pp. 4, 11, 14** · Arregle **PDF pp. 14, 18–20, 23, 36, 39**

Contractor uses it to explain Stage 1: the liability of foreignness is *implicit* in Uppsala but,
in his phrase, "little-explored."

## 5 · Internalization and transaction cost theory

**The idea.** A firm owns foreign production rather than contracting for it when the cost of using
the market exceeds the cost of internal organisation.

Arregle **PDF pp. 18, 20, 23, 25, 32–33, 36, 39–40** · transaction cost at Arregle **pp. 13, 32–33,
37–39**, Contractor **p. 4**, Mezias **p. 2**

## 6 · Bifurcation bias — the newest of these

**Arregle et al. (2021)** · **PDF pp. 8, 13–15, 18–19, 22–23, 31**

A family firm treats family-linked assets as heritage and non-family assets as commodities,
distorting its own resource decisions. It is the family-firm-specific mechanism the review builds
much of its integrative framework on. Worth knowing because it is **recent, named, and testable** —
the kind of construct a project can be built around.

## 7 · Socioemotional wealth

**Arregle** **PDF pp. 23, 34–39.** Non-financial value a family derives from control — identity,
continuity, reputation. It explains why family firms make internationalisation choices that look
irrational financially.

## 8 · The seven IB themes — a map of the whole field's questions

**Arregle et al. (2021)** · **PDF p. 6**

1. Scale (depth) of internationalization
2. Scope (breadth)
3. Entry mode choice
4. Location choice
5. Internationalization process
6. Timing — pace and rhythm
7. Internationalization performance

Chosen because they *"cut broadly across most IB frameworks."* **Use this as the checklist for any
IB project idea:** which of the seven are you actually asking about?

## 9 · The field's own structure

- **Chandra & Newburry (1997)** — IB as the overlap of five disciplines, integrating only on
  strategy. **Figure 1, journal p. 397**
- **Rugman & Verbeke** — firm-specific vs country-specific advantages; regional vs global strategy
- **Integration–Responsiveness framework** — global standardisation against local adaptation
  pressure. Cultural industries need more adaptation
- **Knickerbocker (1973)** — oligopolistic reaction; firms follow rivals across borders

## 10 · Emerging markets

Definition contested — originated with Goldman Sachs' BRIC framing. Debate: size or growth rate?
Variation *within* countries is as large as between them. **Springboard perspective** (Luo & Tung
2007) — emerging-market firms internationalise to *acquire* assets, not to exploit existing ones.
That inverts the classic direction of causation.

# PART TWO · Qualitative Methods — GEB 7911

## 11 · Sensemaking and sensegiving

**Gioia & Chittipeddi (1991)**, *SMJ* 12(6): 433–448

Four phases in a repeating cycle: **envisioning → signalling → re-visioning → energizing**.
Understanding (inward) and influencing others' understanding (outward) alternate rather than
sequence.

**Method contribution:** the **insider/outsider research team** and the **first-order / second-order**
analytic separation. *Verify the phase names against the PDF — that detail is not yet checked
against the paper.*

## 12 · Rhythm in distributed work

**Maznevski & Chudoba (2000)**, *Organization Science* 11(5): 473–492

Three global virtual teams, one multinational, ~21 months. Unit of analysis is the **interaction
incident**. Effective teams keep a rhythm — intense face-to-face meetings set the tempo, lower-
intensity mediated work fills the gaps.

## 13 · The five qualitative approaches

**Creswell & Poth (2024), 5th ed.** — narrative · phenomenology · grounded theory · ethnography ·
case study.

Gonzalez's steer, from the first residency: **phenomenology** suits a phenomenon spread across
organisations with no single site, and suits translating an existing quantitative question.
**Grounded theory** is the most rigorous, and can be used for analysis *inside* another approach.

## 14 · Bias in qualitative work, and how it is managed

Her four named techniques: **insider/outsider pairing · triangulation · memoing and journaling ·
constant comparison.** Plus the **Hawthorne effect** — participants change behaviour when observed.

# PART THREE · Your own theory

## 15 · Anchoring and adjustment

**Tversky & Kahneman (1974).** Judgment starts from a reference point and adjusts insufficiently
away from it. In audit: the prior-year conclusion is the anchor.

## 16 · The three-link chain

| | | Whose failure |
|---|---|---|
| **L1** | System output becomes the anchor — automatic, continuous, arriving before the reviewer forms a view | **Automation bias** — human |
| **L2** | The model agrees with a stated position rather than challenging it | **Sycophancy** — model behaviour |
| **L3** | Successive systems reprocess earlier machine-influenced work and converge on each other rather than on evidence | **Epistemic drift** — system-level |

**Only L1 is a cognitive bias.** Getting that distinction right is what keeps the argument standing
under examination.

## 17 · Where these connect

- **Contractor's diagnosis is yours.** A field disagreeing because it sampled different segments of
  one curve is a field with a measurement problem, not a theory problem
- **Mezias is your method model.** Find the observable trace that isolates one disadvantage
- **Ghemawat's CAGE** gives the cross-national dimension your model currently lacks
- **Arregle's theme 7** is Contractor's question asked of another population, still unresolved after
  220 studies
