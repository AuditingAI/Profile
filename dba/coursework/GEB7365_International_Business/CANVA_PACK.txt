  ############  SLIDE 5 -- THE CONCLUSION  ############

  [EYEBROW]
CONCLUSION -- ALL FOUR

  [HEADING]
What was measured, and what they thought they had measured.

  [FOUR-ROW MAPPING. Left = paper, middle = measured, arrow, right = believed.]
  CONTRACTOR    segments of one curve    ->   the shape of the relationship
  MEZIAS        aggregate performance    ->   the cost of foreignness
  GHEMAWAT      market size              ->   opportunity
  ARREGLE       220 studies              ->   a settled question

  [PULL QUOTE - gold box, italic, large]
One diagnosis, four times: the field measured the wrong thing -- or measured one thing while believing it had measured another.

  [CAPTION]
Every one of them made progress by changing WHAT was measured, not by collecting more of the same thing. A literature that cannot converge because it cannot agree on a measure has a methods problem, not a theory problem.

  1 - THE BEST NUMBER IN THE MODULE                        Arregle, p.6
      Arregle breaks the 220 studies down by theme, with percentages:
        scale 39% | scope 8% | entry mode 18% | location 3%
        process 16% | pace and rhythm 4% | PERFORMANCE 12%

      >> Only 12% of 220 studies address performance -- Contractor's
         question -- and it is STILL unresolved after thirty years.

      SAY THIS ON SLIDE 6, immediately before the Kundu line. It turns
      "still unresolved" from an assertion into arithmetic, and nobody
      else will have opened the paper far enough to find it.

  2 - STAR TV WAS WORSE THAN "LOSSES"                      Ghemawat, p.2
      FY ending 30 June 1999: Star lost $141 million pretax
      on revenues of $111 million.

      >> The losses exceeded the revenue. If Daniela does not use it,
         you can -- it is one line and it lands.
      ($825m paid 1993-95, ~$500m total losses FY1996-99: both confirmed.)

  3 - GHEMAWAT NAMES THE MISTAKE HIMSELF                   Ghemawat, p.4
      "Ignoring cultural distance was one of Star TV's biggest mistakes.
       By supposing that Asian viewers would be happy with English-language
       programming, the company assumed that the TV business was
       insensitive to culture."
