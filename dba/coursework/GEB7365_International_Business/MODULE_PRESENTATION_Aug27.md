## The four articles — all read, all confirmed

### 1 · Contractor, Kundu & Hsu (2003) · *JIBS* 34(1): 5–18
**A three-stage theory of international expansion: the link between multinationality and performance
in the service sector**

**The claim.** The multinationality–performance relationship is **sigmoid (S-shaped)**, not linear,
not a simple U. Three stages:

| Stage | Slope | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Negative | Liability of foreignness, initial learning costs, insufficient scale |
| **2** | Positive | Expansion benefits — scale, scope, learning — outweigh the costs |
| **3** | Negative again | Organisational cost and complexity of managing widely scattered operations |

**Their explanation for why the literature disagrees, in their own words:** studies find positive
linear, negative linear, U-shaped and inverted-U results because *"the data may be capturing only
part of an overall sigmoid function… Depending on which part of Figure 1 we examined, we can find
linear (positive and negative), U-shaped, and inverted-U-shaped segments."*

**That single sentence is the spine of the whole session.** The field was not disagreeing about
firms. It was sampling different segments of one curve.

**The sub-finding worth raising:** knowledge-based service firms (advertising, market research,
securities, publishing) over-expand into the suboptimal Stage 3 more readily than capital-intensive
ones (airlines, hotels, construction, retail), because their fixed-asset risk is lower. And:
*"Few companies possess the managerial tools that would tell them when they have over-internationalized."*

### 2 · Mezias (2002) · *SMJ* 23(3): 229–244
**Identifying liabilities of foreignness and strategies to minimize their effects: the case of labor
lawsuit judgments in the United States**

**The move.** Everyone agreed liability of foreignness existed; nobody could isolate it, because
performance measures aggregate advantages and disadvantages together and transfer pricing
contaminates them. Mezias found an observable trace that does neither: **US labour lawsuit
judgments**.

**Design.** 486 British, German and Japanese subsidiaries in the US, matched against **486 US-owned
firms in the same industries and cities**. Then, within the foreign subsample, he tests whether
staffing strategy, subsidiary autonomy, number of affiliated US firms, and subsidiary age change
exposure.

**Why the measure works, in his words:** *"while most performance measures aggregate advantages and
disadvantages, labor lawsuits only measure labor-related disadvantage. Also, income shifting or
transfer pricing between parent and subsidiary do not affect this measure."*

**The stakes are real money:** employee plaintiffs won 78.9% of defamation cases, 70.0% of sexual
discrimination and harassment cases, 58.4% of wrongful discharge. Average jury award over $600,000;
legal fees averaging $100,000 to trial.

### 3 · Ghemawat (2001) · *HBR* 79(8): 137–147
**Distance still matters: the hard reality of global expansion**

**The claim.** Firms overestimate foreign markets because the standard tool —
**country portfolio analysis** — looks only at GDP, consumer wealth and propensity to consume.
*"It ignores the costs and risks of doing business in a new market. Most of those costs and risks
result from barriers created by distance."*

**Distance is four things, not one — the CAGE framework:** **C**ultural · **A**dministrative or
political · **G**eographic · **E**conomic.

**The case that opens it:** Star TV. Murdoch's News Corporation paid $825m for it in 1993–95 on the
theory that satellite delivery sidestepped geographic distance and English-language programming
sidestepped the rest. Losses of roughly **$500 million** across FY1996–99. Geographic distance was
the one kind they had solved.

### 4 · Arregle, Chirico, Kano, Kundu, Majocchi & Schulze (2021) · *JIBS* 52: 1159–1198
**Family firm internationalization: past research and an agenda for the future**

**Scale.** A systematic review of **220 studies** across three decades.

**The problem it names, verbatim:** existing research *"has offered varied and at times incompatible
findings on how family ownership and management shape internationalization."*

**The organising move.** Seven IB themes, chosen because they cut across most IB frameworks:
(1) scale/depth · (2) scope/breadth · (3) entry mode choice · (4) location choice ·
(5) internationalization process · (6) timing, pace and rhythm · (7) internationalization performance.

**Why it matters here:** theme 7 is Contractor's question, asked of a different population — and the
findings are *incompatible*. Same pattern, thirty years later, different literature.

**The agenda:** cross-disciplinary, multi-theoretic, cross-level; more attention to micro-level
affective motivations, background social institutions, temporal perspectives, and multi-level
analysis.

## The spine — build the whole thing on this

> **All four papers are about the same failure: the field kept measuring the wrong thing, or
> measuring one thing while believing it had measured another.**

- **Contractor** — the field measured segments of a curve and reported them as different relationships
- **Mezias** — the field measured aggregate performance and believed it had measured foreignness
- **Ghemawat** — practitioners measured market size and believed they had measured opportunity
- **Arregle** — thirty years and 220 studies still produce *"varied and at times incompatible findings"*

**That is not four topics. It is one diagnosis, four times.**

## The write-up — his four required components

### 1 · Major findings
The M–P relationship and its contested shape. Contractor's three stages as the reconciling account.
Mezias giving liability of foreignness an independent measure. Ghemawat naming what "distance"
actually contains. Arregle showing the same irreconcilability persists in an adjacent literature.

### 2 · Importance and contribution to IB
If the relationship is sigmoid, *"should we internationalise"* is the wrong question and *"where are
we on the curve"* is the right one. Contractor makes the point himself: **few firms have the
managerial tools to know when they have over-expanded.** That is a contribution to practice, not
only theory.

### 3 · Limitations — where the marks are
**Measurement is the through-line, and you can now say it with evidence from the papers themselves.**

- Contractor states outright that contradictory findings arise from sampling different segments of
  one curve — so the disagreement was partly artefactual
- "Degree of internationalisation" is operationalised many ways — foreign sales ratio, foreign
  assets, country count, entropy, TNI — and the visible shape depends on the choice
- Mezias is the counter-example that proves the rule: he made progress by **abandoning aggregate
  performance measures entirely** for one that isolates a single disadvantage
- Arregle, thirty years and 220 studies later, still reports incompatible findings

Also: survivorship (failed internationalisers exit the sample), endogeneity (profitable firms may
internationalise rather than the reverse), and Contractor's sample being service-sector firms in one
period.

### 4 · Connections
- **Forward, to entry mode (3 Sep):** if Stage 1 is negative, mode choice is a decision about how
  much of that early cost to absorb at once
- **Forward, to institutions and culture (19 Sep):** Ghemawat's CAGE is a pre-Hofstede way of saying
  what that session will say with Hofstede and GLOBE
- **Back, to residency 1:** Rugman and Verbeke's firm-specific advantages are what carry a firm
  through Stage 1 at all
- **Across the room:** **Sumit K. Kundu of FIU is an author on both Contractor (2003) and Arregle
  (2021)** — eighteen years apart, same question about performance, still unresolved. Worth naming
  out loud
- **To your own work:** a literature that cannot agree because it cannot agree on a measure has a
  methods problem, not a theory problem. That is the argument you are already making
