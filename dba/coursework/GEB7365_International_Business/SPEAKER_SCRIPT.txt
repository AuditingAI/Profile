  SLIDE 2  --  CONTRACTOR'S CURVE                             ~70 sec
================================================================================

  POINT AT THE CURVE. Let it do the work.

  SAY:
  "For thirty years this literature reported four different answers. Positive.
   Negative. U-shaped. Inverted-U. All with real data.

   Contractor, Kundu and Hsu say they were all looking at the same curve.
   It's sigmoid. Three stages.

   Stage one is negative -- liability of foreignness, learning costs, no scale
   yet. Stage two turns positive as scale and learning outweigh the cost.
   Stage three goes negative again because managing scattered operations
   gets expensive.

   Here's the line that matters." [PAUSE]

  "Sample only the middle, and you publish a positive linear relationship.
   Sample the ends, you publish an inverted U. The disagreement was partly
   artefactual. The field wasn't arguing about firms -- it was sampling
   different pieces of one curve."

  ONE NUMBER TO REMEMBER: none. This slide is a picture.


================================================================================
  SLIDE 3  --  THE PRACTICE LINE                              ~55 sec
================================================================================

  SAY:
  "There's a second finding that gets less attention and I think matters more.

   Knowledge-based service firms -- advertising, market research, securities --
   push into that bad third stage more readily than capital-intensive firms
   like airlines or hotels. Lower fixed assets, so one more market is easy to
   say yes to.

   And then Contractor writes this." [READ THE QUOTE OFF THE SLIDE]

   "'Few companies possess the managerial tools that would tell them when they
    have over-internationalized.'

   So if the relationship really is sigmoid, then 'should we internationalise?'
   is the wrong question. 'Where are we on the curve?' is the right one --
   and almost nobody can answer it."


================================================================================
  SLIDE 4  --  MEZIAS                                         ~70 sec
================================================================================

  SAY:
  "Mezias is the craft lesson.

   Everybody agreed liability of foreignness existed. Nobody could isolate it.
   Why? Because performance measures roll advantages and disadvantages into one
   number, and transfer pricing contaminates whatever's left.

   So he stops trying to fix the measure. He finds a different one entirely --
   US labour lawsuit judgments." [POINT AT THE QUOTE]

  "'Labor lawsuits only measure labor-related disadvantage.'

   The design is 486 foreign subsidiaries matched against 486 US-owned firms,
   same industries, same cities. Average jury award over six hundred thousand
   dollars, so this is real money, not a proxy nobody cares about.

   The point isn't the lawsuits. It's that he did not improve the measurement
   of performance. He abandoned it."

  THREE NUMBERS: 486. 486. $600k.


================================================================================
  SLIDE 5  --  THE CONCLUSION                                 ~80 sec
================================================================================

  THIS IS YOUR SLIDE. Slow down here.

  SAY:
  "So let me pull all four together, Daniela's included.

   These aren't four topics. They're one diagnosis, four times." [PAUSE]

  "At each stage the field measured the wrong thing -- or measured one thing
   while believing it had measured another.

   Contractor measured segments of a curve and reported them as different
   relationships.

   Mezias's field measured aggregate performance and believed it had measured
   foreignness.

   Ghemawat's practitioners measured market size and believed they had measured
   opportunity.

   And Arregle -- thirty years, two hundred and twenty studies -- still reports
   'varied and at times incompatible findings.'

   Every one of them made progress by changing WHAT was measured. Not by
   collecting more of the same thing.

   A literature that can't converge because it can't agree on a measure has a
   methods problem, not a theory problem."
