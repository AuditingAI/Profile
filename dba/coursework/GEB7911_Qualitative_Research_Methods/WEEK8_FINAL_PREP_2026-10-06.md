## The spine: if you remember nothing else

> **When an AI tool agrees with an auditor, a human tendency and a machine property point the same
> way. Measuring reliance cannot tell them apart. Asking auditors what that moment is like can.**

## Numbers to have cold

| Fact | Figure |
|---|---|
| Eligibility | 4 criteria · **5+ years** · recurring engagements · uses AI tools · **can recall a specific occasion** |
| Sample | **10 to 15**, decided by depth and richness, not saturation |
| Interview | one per person, **about 60 minutes**, semi-structured, recorded with consent |
| Why interviews, not a survey | a panel of **334,976** returned **about 20** eligible (about six per hundred thousand); 23 raw responses, **4 usable** |
| Firm cap | **no more than 2** participants per firm |
| Stopping guide | rich enough for textural and structural descriptions **+** 3 consecutive interviews with no new meaning unit **+** reviewer agrees |

## The talk, about 8 minutes

| # | Poster box | Time | Say this, in your words |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hook | 0:30 | "Every auditor on a recurring engagement starts with last year's conclusion. Now there is a second voice in the room: an AI tool that reaches a conclusion too. My study is about the moment that tool agrees with you." |
| 2 | Problem | 1:00 | "Firms and regulators are writing AI policies on the assumption that human review is a working control. Research has focused mostly on *how much* auditors rely on AI output, by experiment and archive. Much less is known about what it is like to receive a machine's confirmation of a judgment you already hold. That is the gap." |
| 3 | Purpose and RQ | 1:00 | Read the RQ slowly, word for word: *"How do experienced auditors experience and make sense of receiving an AI-generated conclusion that confirms a judgment they had already formed?"* Then: "Notice the word *receiving*, not *relying*. Relying assumes the answer." |
| 4 | Assumptions | 1:00 | "Social constructivist. There are as many accounts of this moment as auditors who lived it. And I am not neutral: I spent my career in audit and risk at Citi and JPMorgan, so I may hear confirmation where there is none. That is why I keep a **confirmation hazard log**, and every entry records what I did about it." |
| 5 | Approach | 0:45 | "Phenomenology, because the question is about lived experience and meaning. Not grounded theory: I am not studying a process. Not case study: the phenomenon crosses firms, it has no bounded site." |
| 6 | Data collection | 1:30 | "Criterion sampling. The fourth criterion carries the study: you must be able to recall a specific occasion. Recall, not interpretation; the meaning comes out in the interview. I test the screening question first, add referrals screened the same way, and stop on depth, not on a saturation rule." |
| 7 | Analysis | 1:00 | "Creswell's five activities. Horizontalization, meaning units, themes in participants' language, a textural description of *what*, a structural description of *how*, then the essence. Before I write the essence I re-read every transcript only for what contradicts it. I code by hand; NVivo stores the codes; AI does not assign them, because that would recreate the phenomenon inside my analysis." |
| 8 | Validation and ethics | 1:00 | "Three lenses. Participants check their own description only, not my composite. An outside reviewer reads a few purposefully chosen transcripts, including one that challenges me, with my memos. And the hazard log, tied to the audit trail. Consent, pseudonyms, encrypted storage, nobody approached before IRB." |
| 9 | Contributions and limits | 0:45 | "For research: it separates automation bias from sycophancy at the one moment they look identical. For practice: it tests, from the inside, whether human review is the control policies assume. Limits: small sample, recalled experience, no generalization claimed." |
| 10 | Close | 0:15 | "The machine agreeing with you feels like evidence. My study asks whether it is experienced that way, and what auditors do next. Thank you." Then one line: "As disclosed on the slide, I used AI to help lay out this poster; the research decisions are mine." |

## Questions to expect, and your answer in one breath

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Why not just survey them? | "I tried. A 335,000-member panel gave about twenty eligible people. And a survey asks people to notice the very thing the bias stops them noticing." |
| Isn't criterion 4 too narrow? | "Dr. Gonzalez and I discussed it. Broadening it would weaken the focus. The risk is feasibility, so I handle it in recruitment: test the screen, use referrals." |
| How do you know they had the experience and aren't giving opinions? | "The screen asks for one specific occasion. A general 'all the time' with no episode behind it is not a yes." |
| You're an insider. How do you control bias? | "I don't claim to remove it. I make it visible: memo before each listen-back, leading questions flagged, the hazard log with what I did, and an outside reviewer." |
| Why 10 to 15? | "Depth over count. Below 10 I don't stop; I decide on richness, record why, and the reviewer has to agree." |
| What if they say AI agreement makes them check *harder*? | "Then that is my finding. I wrote down in advance what would prove me wrong; that is one of them." |
| Automation bias vs sycophancy? | "Automation bias is in the person. Sycophancy is in the model. When the tool agrees with you, both push the same way, and only the person's account can separate them." |
| Why "make sense of"? Isn't that a second question? | "In phenomenology, the meaning someone makes of an experience is part of the experience, not a separate process." |
| Will you use AI in the analysis? | "Not to code, name themes or interpret. It would reproduce the very thing I'm studying. Every use is logged and disclosed." |
| How does this connect to your dissertation? | "Same chain, different link. The dissertation asks *how much* by survey; this asks *how* by interview." |
| Internal or external auditors? | Say whichever you settled in the paper. Recommended: "Both, deliberately, for variation, capped at two per firm." |

**If you don't know:** "That's a good challenge, and I haven't resolved it yet. Here is how I'd approach it…" Then
name the step. Never apologize; name the next step.

## Delivery

- Open without a preamble. First words are the hook, not "so, um, my study is…"
- Slow down on the RQ. It is the one sentence they must hear exactly.
- Point at the poster box as you speak to it; it keeps the class with you.
- Land the closing line and stop. Silence after it is confidence.
