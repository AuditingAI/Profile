# Qualitative Research Proposal — skeleton

**GEB 7911 · Research Proposal = 50% of the course grade · built across eight weeks, presented Week 8**

Structured on Dr. Gonzalez's own seven-part framework, given in the first residency:

> Research problem → Research question → Context → Systematic approach → Data collection →
> Data analysis → Complex account

*Source: first-residency meeting notes, 21 Aug 2026. Where this document and those notes disagree,
the notes govern.*

---

## Three constraints that shape everything below

1. **The dissertation stays quantitative.** Her words: *"Dissertation will remain quantitative for
   this program; qualitative is for the course proposal only."* Mixed methods is explicitly not
   recommended. This proposal runs *alongside* the dissertation, not inside it.
2. **The IRB does not change.** *"IRB process is primarily wired for quantitative; changing
   methodology mid-study is highly restricted."* The qualitative data comes from an **open-ended
   text field on the existing survey** — her own suggested route, which changes neither the
   methodology nor the IRB classification.
3. **Watch the vocabulary.** She noted that *"AI tools often incorrectly introduce quantitative
   language (e.g. 'factors') when generating qualitative research questions."* Before submitting,
   search the document for: **factors · variables · interventions · mediators · constructs · effect ·
   impact · influence · determine · cause · relate**. None of them belong here.

## 1 · Research problem

*What is not known, and why it matters to practice.*

Prior work — including my own qualifying study — treats organizational safeguards against anchoring
as things whose strength can be measured. What is not known is how auditors actually experience
those safeguards when the anchor is no longer a prior-year workpaper but a machine-generated
conclusion that arrives before they have formed a view, and that agrees with them.

**Write ~400 words. Anchor it in practice, not in the literature gap alone.**

## 2 · Research question

**Central question**

> How do experienced auditors at firms using AI-assisted review navigate reliance on
> system-generated conclusions while working multi-year engagements?

**Subquestions** — five to seven, all opening with *how* or *what*:

1. What do auditors describe happening when a system confirms a judgment they had already reached?
2. How do auditors distinguish, in their own accounts, between confirmation and corroboration?
3. What do auditors describe doing to test a conclusion they agree with?
4. How do auditors describe the point at which they stop examining a system output?
5. What distinctions do auditors draw between a system conclusion and a prior-year workpaper as a
   starting point?
6. How do reviewers describe evaluating work they know was AI-assisted?
7. What do auditors describe changing in their own practice across successive engagements?

*Subquestion 5 is the hinge — it connects this study to the dissertation's territory without
importing its vocabulary.*

## 3 · Context

*Qualitative findings are context-dependent. The setting is part of the claim.*

- Multi-year audit engagements, where a prior conclusion is available to be carried forward
- Firms with AI-assisted review tools in live use — not pilots
- Experienced auditors: enough tenure to have worked engagements both before and after these tools
- US practice, PCAOB/AICPA/IIA standards environment

**State plainly that findings are not generalisable** and are not intended to be. That is the point
she made most often in the first session.

## 4 · Systematic approach

**Phenomenology**, with grounded-theory techniques for the analysis.

Her own steer, from the residency: *"Phenomenology studies a phenomenon across individuals or
organizations without needing a single site; good fit for translating existing quantitative research
questions."* That describes this study exactly — a phenomenon distributed across firms, with no
single site available.

She also noted approaches can be combined, and that grounded theory *"can be used purely for
rigorous data analysis within other approaches."* She is a grounded theory specialist; saying this
deliberately, rather than by accident, will read as having listened.

**Rejected, and say why in one line each:**
- *Ethnography* — requires embedded access to a firm's review process that is not obtainable
- *Case study* — would need a bounded site; the phenomenon crosses firms
- *Narrative* — she noted a lower validity bar for business research
- *Grounded theory alone* — theoretical sampling to saturation is not feasible in this population

## 5 · Data collection

**Primary — open-ended responses on the existing instrument.** The survey already carries an
open-text item asking respondents to describe an occasion when an automated or AI-generated figure
influenced their judgment. This is her recommended route and it leaves the IRB untouched.

**Sampling — purposive, criterion-based.** Not random, and not a general research panel. Criteria:
tenure, multi-year engagement experience, live use of AI-assisted review.

**On the recruitment constraint, and how the proposal should treat it.** Screening a commercial
panel of 334,976 returned approximately twenty eligible people — near six per hundred thousand. That
is a finding about *panel recruitment*, not about the population's existence. Purposive sampling
draws from professional networks, association chapters and referral chains instead, which is a
different surface entirely. **Write this into the proposal as a reasoned sampling strategy — it is
one of the strongest sections available, because it is evidenced rather than assumed.**

**Triangulation** — she named this repeatedly as the main defence against bias and against the
Hawthorne effect. Add a second source: anonymised firm methodology guidance, published inspection
findings, or documented AI-use policies.

## 6 · Data analysis

- **First-order** codes in participants' own words → **second-order** themes in researcher
  constructs → aggregate dimensions
- **Constant comparison** — collection and analysis run in parallel, not in sequence
- **Memoing and a reflexive journal** throughout, kept as evidence rather than as habit

**Do the first pass by hand.** Her AI policy is that AI is permitted generally but she will say when
not to use it, *because students must learn qualitative analysis manually first in order to evaluate
AI output critically*. Coding is exactly that kind of exercise.

## 7 · Complex account

Findings written as a **rich narrative**, not tables. She made the point that qualitative articles
run long precisely because the account carries the evidence.

Target output: a process account of how reliance forms, hardens, and occasionally breaks — with the
route from what was observed to what is claimed visible on the page.

## Reflexivity — write this section early, not last

I am an auditor of fifteen years studying auditors. That is an insider position and it cuts both
ways: access and interpretive fluency on one side, assumed shared meaning and unexamined framing on
the other.

Techniques, all four named by her in the first session:

| Technique | How it applies here |
|---|---|
| **Insider/outsider pairing** | The design Gioia & Chittipeddi used, and she cited it in class |
| **Triangulation** | A documentary source alongside participant accounts |
| **Memoing / journaling** | A dated reflexive record, kept from the first code onward |
| **Constant comparison** | Forces each new account against the emerging pattern rather than the reverse |

The **Hawthorne effect** should be named explicitly — auditors describing their own use of AI tools
to a researcher have an obvious incentive to describe careful practice.
