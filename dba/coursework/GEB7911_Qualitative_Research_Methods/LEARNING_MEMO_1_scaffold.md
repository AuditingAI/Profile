## The angle worth writing — it is yours and nobody else in the room has it

Most first memos will say qualitative research is valuable because it captures context. True, and
generic.

**You have something better: you ran into the wall from the other side, this year, with your own
money.**

Your qualifying study specified an eleven-construct model and needed n=100 auditors with long-tenure
engagement experience. Applying the eligibility criteria to a commercial panel of **334,976 people
returned roughly 20 eligible** — about six per hundred thousand. You ended with **four valid
responses**. Not one hypothesis could be tested.

That is not a story about bad luck. It is a story about **a method whose logic requires a population
size that the population does not have.** And the obvious counterfactual is the whole point of this
course: *twenty people is a catastrophic sample for a survey and a perfectly respectable one for an
interview study.* The same twenty humans that make a quantitative design impossible make a
qualitative design viable.

That reframing — that method choice is not a preference but a function of what the population can
actually supply — is a genuine insight, it is first-hand, and it directly answers all three of her
prompts. It is also honest about a failure, which in a doctoral seminar reads as maturity rather
than weakness.

**One caution:** do not let it become a complaint about Prolific. The memo should land on what you
now understand about matching design to population, not on what went wrong.

end of the term.

## Questions to think through before you write

Answer these for yourself. The memo is the residue.

**On the contrast:**
- Your survey needed 100 people to say something about auditors. How many people does a case study
  need, and *why is that not cheating*? What does it claim instead?
- Your 55 Likert items fixed in advance what could be observed. What can an interview find that a
  fixed instrument definitionally cannot?
- Where does a qualitative study's credibility come from, if not from sample size and p-values?

**On the readings — pick the one that gives you the sharpest contrast:**
- **Gioia & Chittipeddi (1991)** — a single organisation, studied from inside, during a change
  process. Ask: what is the unit of analysis, and how do they defend a claim built from one site?
  Their coding approach became a widely used method; ask what makes it *systematic* rather than
  impressionistic. That question is the heart of the contrast.
- **Kirtley & O'Mahony (2023)** — inductive theory-building across multiple cases, longitudinal.
  Ask: how do they get from what firms did to a general construct? Where exactly does theory enter?
- **Maznevski & Chudoba (2000)** — a longitudinal study of teams over time. Ask: what does watching
  something unfold reveal that measuring it once cannot? You have a mediation model that assumes a
  temporal ordering you never observed. Sit with that.

**On your own work:**
- Your dissertation extension — automated anchoring → sycophantic confirmation → recursive drift —
  is *argued but untested*, and the third link is the weakest. Which of those three links is
  actually a question about **what people do and why**, rather than how much of something there is?
- If you interviewed fifteen auditors who use AI review tools, what would you learn that 100 survey
  responses would not?

**Read these first, in the textbook:** Creswell & Poth (2024), 5th ed. — the opening chapters on
what characterises qualitative inquiry and on choosing among the five approaches. That is where the
vocabulary she wants you using comes from.
