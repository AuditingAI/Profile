## The three Week 5 papers tell one methodological story

All three are inductive studies in which the researcher's starting category was **wrong, borrowed, or
missing entirely**, and in each case the method is what caught it.

| | **Gerlach & Cenfetelli (2020)** | **Cha & Edmondson (2006)** | **Dane (2020)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The category problem** | The field's category was **borrowed**. "Addiction" was applied, in their words, *"in a confirmatory manner"* | Their own category was **wrong**. They went in to study the **positive** effects of strong values | **No category existed.** Nobody had a vocabulary for how people make sense of an epiphany |
| **Tradition** | Classic Glaser (1978) | Longitudinal single case, emic | **Modified grounded theory**, citing Corbin & Strauss *and* **Gioia et al. (2013)** |
| **Data** | 90 people: 46 interviews + 44 anonymous surveys. 35 hrs, ~680 pp | ~120 hrs field research, two phases **three years apart**. 45 interviews, 41 visits | 128 survey responses → 22 interviews → **26 epiphanies** |
| **Coding ladder** | Open → selective → theoretical | Inductive from observation and interview | First-order data → theoretical categories → **aggregate dimensions** |
| **Result** | IT-mediated state-tracking | The **hypocrisy attribution dynamic** | Four dimensions: Features, Mystery, Serendipity, **Readiness** |

**The line to offer if the room goes quiet:** all three papers are arguments against knowing the
answer before you collect the data, made three different ways. Gerlach refused a category the field
had already assigned. Cha and Edmondson had theirs overturned by what they saw. Dane had to build
one from nothing.

## Four things worth raising, in order of how much they will land

### 1 · Dane asks people to remember. Cha and Edmondson went back and watched.

Dane's epiphanies occurred **on average about ten years before the interview, the oldest twenty-nine
years before.** He is studying retrospective sensemaking about a nonconscious process, through a
single question: *"Would you please tell me about your epiphany in as much detail as possible?"*

Cha and Edmondson did the opposite. Phase 1 in 1994-95, **Phase 2 three years later**, at the same
firm, with eight of the original nine informants still there. They did not ask people to recall
disenchantment. They came back and found it.

**The question for the room:** when the phenomenon is a moment of realisation, is recall evidence of
the moment, or evidence of the story the person now tells about it? Both papers are strong. They
answer that question in opposite directions.

### 2 · Three papers, three different things called "grounded"

Gerlach follows **Glaser**. Dane cites **Corbin and Strauss and Gioia in the same sentence.** Cha and
Edmondson do not claim grounded theory at all. The label covers genuinely different commitments, and
only Gerlach's choice does real work in the argument, because classic Glaserian anti-forcing is
exactly the stick he beats the addiction literature with.

**Worth asking:** does citing two traditions at once, as Dane does, buy rigour or blur it?

### 3 · Nobody reports inter-coder reliability

Gerlach has two authors. Cha and Edmondson had research assistants. **Dane worked alone and writes
in the first person throughout: "I read each interview transcript multiple times."**

None of the three reports an agreement statistic. In each case the defence is different, and that is
the interesting part rather than a gotcha.

### 4 · The protocol changed in all three, and each says so

Gerlach added questions as constant checking emerged. **Dane noticed participants would not credit
themselves, and began asking later interviewees specifically about "credit."** Cha and Edmondson
changed the entire research question between phases.

**That is theoretical sampling doing visible work**, and it is the single clearest contrast with the
quantitative instrument I fielded, which was frozen before the first response arrived and could not
have learned anything.

## The contribution only you can make

Your own proposal asks auditors about **the moment of receiving an AI conclusion that matches one
they had already formed.** Point 1 above is not an abstract methods question for you, it is your
design.

> "Dane's participants are recalling something that happened on average ten years earlier. My
> participants would be recalling a moment that may have lasted seconds and that they had no reason
> to mark at the time. Cha and Edmondson's answer was to go back three years later and observe. I
> cannot observe an auditor's judgment forming. So what is the phenomenological equivalent, and is
> recall good enough evidence for a moment nobody flagged while it was happening?"
