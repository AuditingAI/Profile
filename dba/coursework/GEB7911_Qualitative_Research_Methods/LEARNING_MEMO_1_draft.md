**Learning Memo 1 — Getting Started**
Yasir A. Malik · GEB 7911 · 20 August 2026

I came to this course having just finished a study that could not be finished. That sounds like a
complaint. It is the most useful thing that has happened in my doctoral work, and it is why the
distinction between qualitative and quantitative inquiry is not abstract to me.

My qualifying study specified an eleven-construct model with fifty-five Likert items and needed
roughly one hundred experienced auditors. Applying my eligibility criteria to a commercial research
panel of 334,976 members returned approximately twenty eligible people — a prevalence near six per
hundred thousand. I finished with four valid responses and tested no hypotheses. The responses I did
get were clean; none failed an attention check. The instrument worked. The design did not.

What the readings clarified is that this was not a recruitment failure. It was a mismatch between a
question and a method. A survey's claim to knowledge rests on many people each saying a little.
Twenty people is a catastrophic sample for that and a perfectly respectable one for an interview
study. The same twenty humans who made my design impossible would have made a qualitative design
viable. Method is not a preference; it is a function of what the population can actually supply, and
I never asked what mine could supply before committing the budget.

That is what made Gioia and Chittipeddi (1991) land. They study strategic change initiation inside a
single organization, following one president through one process — an n that would end a
quantitative paper before it began. The paper still makes a defensible theoretical claim, and I
wanted to understand where the defensibility comes from. Not from representativeness, which they
never claim. It comes from the discipline of the analysis: informant-centric accounts held separate
from theory-centric interpretation, so a reader can see where the informants stop and the authors
begin. Creswell and Poth make the same point more generally — rigour here is procedural and visible
rather than statistical. I had assumed qualitative work traded rigour for richness. It trades one
kind of rigour for another, and the second kind is legible on the page in a way a p-value is not.

This changes what I intend to do next. My dissertation extension argues a three-link chain:
automated anchoring in AI-assisted review, then sycophantic confirmation, then recursive drift across
engagements. The first link is a magnitude question and a survey suits it. The middle link is not.
Whether an auditor defers to a machine-generated starting point, and what they tell themselves while
doing it, is a question about reasoning and account-giving. Fifteen interviews would reach it. A
hundred survey responses would report that it happened without showing how.

I want to leave this term able to defend a bounded case the way Gioia and Chittipeddi defend theirs,
and to know which of my questions deserves which method before I spend anything on it.
