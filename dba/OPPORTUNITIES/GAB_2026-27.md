## Should you apply? Yes — and not for the generic reason

**The honest case against, first.** It is a yearlong commitment. Chapters 4–6 are overdue since
28 July, two courses are running, and half the GEB 7365 grade does not exist yet. Adding a
commitment when the existing ones are late is a real risk and worth naming out loud.

**The case for is stronger, and it is specific to you.**

The academic CV's service section is empty. That is the thinnest part of the whole academic track,
and it is the section a department chair reads to find out whether someone contributes to an
institution or only consumes it. Service cannot be manufactured later — it has to have been done.

But the reason this particular board, this particular year:

> **FIU's University Graduate School issued AI guidelines in August 2026.** They require every
> graduate student to disclose AI use, name the tool and purpose, and obtain committee approval.
> The policy exists. What does not exist is anything that helps a graduate student actually comply
> with it.

You are a doctoral candidate whose dissertation is about what happens to professional judgment when
an AI system reaches a conclusion first. GAB's stated purpose is *projects that enhance the graduate
experience.* **The overlap is not a stretch — it is the single best-aligned service opportunity you
are likely to see.**

And the second-order benefit, which is worth saying plainly: the UGS AI policy is the document that
governs your own dissertation, and you currently have an open compliance item under it — the
committee approval in §3.2 that has never been requested. Being in the room where that policy is
discussed is not a conflict; it is the ordinary way a graduate student learns how a policy is meant
to be applied.

## What to write

Most GAB applications ask some version of: why do you want to serve, what would you contribute, and
what would you work on. The third is where applications are won — **almost nobody arrives with a
concrete project.**

### The project to propose

> **A practical AI-use guide for graduate students, built from the UGS guidelines.**
>
> The University Graduate School issued AI guidelines this August. They are clear about what is
> required — disclosure naming the tool and purpose, a statement in the preliminary pages and in the
> Methods section, committee review and approval, and a hard line against uploading identifiable data
> to public platforms.
>
> What is missing is the layer between the policy and the student: a worked disclosure statement a
> student can adapt, guidance on how to raise AI use with a committee before it becomes a problem at
> defence, and a plain answer to the question every student actually has, which is where the line
> falls between organising your own argument and having a model make it for you.
>
> I would work on that, with the office rather than around it. I am a DBA candidate researching
> exactly this boundary — what happens to professional judgment when an AI system reaches a
> conclusion before the human does — and I have been keeping a disclosure record on my own
> dissertation since the guidelines were issued.

**Why this pitch works.** It is specific, it is timely, it credits the office rather than critiquing
it, and it proposes work you would genuinely be good at. Compare it to "I want to give back to the
graduate community," which is what most of the pile will say.

### Why you want to serve

> I came to the DBA from fifteen years in audit and risk at Citigroup and JPMorgan Chase, and before
> that as a state bank examiner. The habit that transfers is a specific one: when a control is
> supposed to be working, someone has to go and check whether it actually does.
>
> Graduate policy is full of controls of that kind, and most of them were written by people who
> genuinely wanted them to work. What is usually missing is the pass that asks whether they land the
> way they were meant to. That is the contribution I can make, and it is a different one from what
> most of the board will bring.

### What you would contribute

> A working practitioner's view. I am part-time, I commute, I am in year two, and I have already hit
> the ordinary failure modes — a data-collection plan that did not survive contact with reality, a
> policy that arrived mid-course, deadlines that assume a full-time student. Those are the experiences
> a board needs represented and they are usually the ones missing from it.
