# Protocol — a phenomenological study of auditor reliance on agreeing AI

**Yasir A. Malik · FIU DBA · drafted 26 August 2026 · not yet reviewed by Dr. Gonzalez**

*Method after Creswell & Poth (2024), ch. 4 (phenomenology as an approach) and ch. 8 (analysis).
Chapter numbers are `[VERIFY]` against the 5th edition before this text enters the proposal.*

---

## 1 · The central question

> **How do experienced auditors describe and account for their reliance on AI-generated conclusions
> when the system's output agrees with a position they have already formed?**

Carried unchanged from `../00_Execution/QUALITATIVE_REFRAME_L2_L3.md` §4. It already satisfies the
construction rules the course runs on: opens with *how*, uses an exploratory verb, is
non-directional, and presumes no effect.

**Subquestions** — the seven in that file, unchanged. Subquestion 7 (how auditors distinguish an AI
conclusion from a prior-year workpaper as a starting point) is **the hinge**: it is the only one that
connects this arm directly to the validated anchoring model, and it is the one a committee will ask
about.

---

## 2 · The phenomenon, stated precisely

The phenomenon is **not** "using AI." It is not "trusting AI." It is a specific, bounded experience:

> The moment at which an auditor, having formed a provisional view, encounters a system-generated
> conclusion that agrees with it — and what they then do, notice, and tell themselves.

Every participant must have lived **that** moment. This is what makes the sample criterion-based
rather than convenience-based, and it is the single most common way phenomenological studies go
wrong: recruiting people who have opinions about the phenomenon rather than experience of it.

**Screening consequence:** a candidate who uses AI tools but cannot recall a specific occasion when
the tool agreed with a view they had already reached is **not eligible.** See
`SAMPLING_AND_RECRUITMENT.md` §2.

## 3 · Epoché — the bracketing statement

Phenomenology requires the researcher to set out their own experience of the phenomenon first, so the
reader can see what is being set aside. Mine is not neutral and pretending otherwise would be the
error this study is about.

**What I bring, on the record:**

1. **Fifteen years inside the practice.** Audit and risk at Citigroup and JPMorgan Chase, and a
   period as a bank examiner. I have been the reviewer who agreed with the prior-year conclusion. My
   instinct is to read deference as a control failure, which is a *judgment* about the phenomenon, not
   a description of it.
2. **A prior theoretical commitment.** I built an eleven-construct model that treats anchoring as
   something to be *mitigated*. That model presumes the phenomenon is a problem. A participant who
   experiences AI agreement as legitimate corroboration is not thereby wrong, and my instrument gives
   me no vocabulary for taking them seriously.
3. **A stake in the answer.** The three-link chain is my argument. I would find it convenient if
   auditors described exactly the deference the chain predicts. That is the most dangerous thing on
   this page.
4. **An expensive failure behind me.** The qualifying study could not be fielded. There is pressure
   on this arm to *produce*, and pressure to produce is how a researcher starts hearing what they
   need to hear.

**What bracketing means in practice here** — not "having no views," which is not achievable, but four
specific procedures:

| Procedure | When |
|---|---|
| Write the reflexive memo **before** listening back to each interview | Every interview, no exceptions |
| Flag in the transcript margin every place I asked a leading question | During transcription |
| The outsider reviewer (see §5) reads for **my** presence in the data | Every third transcript |
| Record any moment I felt confirmed, and treat it as a hazard flag | `AUDIT_TRAIL.md`, same day |

Item 4 is the one that matters. **Feeling confirmed by a participant is the researcher-side instance
of the very phenomenon under study.** If it is not logged, this study is a demonstration of its own
argument rather than an examination of it.

## 4 · The analytic sequence

Modified Stevick–Colaizzi–Keen, as set out in Creswell & Poth. Each step has a stopping condition, so
the analysis has finish lines rather than running until it feels done.

| # | Step | Output | Done when |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Epoché revisited** | Updated bracketing memo | The memo names at least one view that changed since §3 |
| 2 | **Horizontalisation** | Every significant statement listed, flat, none ranked | The transcript yields no further statement about the phenomenon |
| 3 | **Reduction to meaning units** | Non-overlapping, non-repetitive statements | No two units say the same thing |
| 4 | **Clustering into themes** | Named clusters, each traceable to its units | Every unit sits in exactly one cluster, or is explicitly parked |
| 5 | **Textural description** | *What* participants experienced, in their words | Written entirely from clustered units, quoted |
| 6 | **Structural description** | *How* and *in what context* it was experienced — imaginative variation | Each textural element has a context attached |
| 7 | **Composite / essence** | One synthesis: the invariant structure | It holds for every participant, or the exception is stated |

**Steps 5 and 6 are the study.** Step 7 is where phenomenologies overreach — the essence is a
description of what was shared, not a theory of what causes it. If step 7 starts producing causal
language, it has stopped being phenomenology and become an untested variance model in prose.

## 5 · The insider/outsider reflexivity design

Borrowed from Gioia & Chittipeddi (1991) — **their design, not their analytic method.**

| Role | Who | What they do |
|---|---|---|
| **Insider** | Yasir | Recruits, interviews, transcribes, codes. Deep domain fluency; deep prior commitment |
| **Outsider** | A reader with no stake in the chain — `[VERIFY: recruit; a GEB 7911 cohort member or Dr. Gonzalez's designee]` | Reads transcripts and coded output cold. Does not attend interviews. Asks one question: *where is the researcher in this data?* |

The insider's fluency is what makes the interviews good — an auditor will not open up to someone who
needs "tickmark" explained. That same fluency is what makes the analysis dangerous. The design does
not resolve the tension; it makes it visible to a second person.

**This arrangement is simultaneously the epistemological and the axiological position of the study,**
and `TRUSTWORTHINESS.md` treats it as such.

---

## 6 · What would make this study wrong

Stated in advance, because a design that cannot be disconfirmed is not a design.

| If participants describe… | Then |
|---|---|
| Systematically **checking harder** when a system agrees, not less | The L2 argument is weakened at its core and the finding is that agreement triggers scrutiny |
| No experiential difference between an AI conclusion and a prior-year workpaper | Subquestion 7 collapses, and the chain's novelty claim goes with it |
| Treating AI agreement as **corroboration they then test**, with named tests | The phenomenon exists but is already governed, and the practical contribution shrinks to documenting the controls |
| Nothing recognisable as a moment at all — the tool is ambient | The phenomenon as framed does not exist in practice and the question must be rebuilt |

Any of these is a real result and gets written up as one. The programme's stated failure mode is
overstatement; this table is the guard against it.

---

## 7 · What this protocol does not cover

- **L3.** Out of scope. See `README.md`.
- **Any quantitative claim.** No counts of participants who said X. Phenomenology does not license
  frequency claims, and reporting them is how qualitative work gets dismissed.
- **Generalisation to auditors at large.** Transferability is offered through thick description; the
  reader judges the fit. See `TRUSTWORTHINESS.md` §2.
