# Interview guide

**Semi-structured · 60 minutes · recorded with consent · one participant at a time**

The guide is a resource, not a script. Phenomenological interviewing follows the participant into
their experience; a question asked because it is next on the list is a question that produces an
opinion instead of a description.

---

## The two questions that carry the study

Creswell's phenomenological interview reduces to two broad questions. Everything else is a probe.

> **1. What have you experienced when an AI tool reached the same conclusion you had already reached?**
>
> **2. What situations or circumstances have typically influenced how you experienced that?**

Question 1 produces the **textural** description — what happened. Question 2 produces the
**structural** — the context that shaped it. If an interview runs long and only one can be covered
properly, cover 1.

---

## Opening — 5 minutes

Purpose: establish that this is not an examination, and that there is no correct answer.

> "I'm studying how auditors experience working alongside AI tools — not whether the tools are
> accurate, and not whether anyone is using them correctly. There's nothing to get right here. I'm
> interested in what it's actually like.
>
> I'll ask you to think of specific occasions rather than talk in general terms, because the general
> version is usually the version we've already tidied up.
>
> Everything is confidential, the recording stays with me, nothing identifies you or your firm, and
> you can stop or skip anything at any point."

**Then, before anything else — the grounding move:**

> "Before we start, can you take a moment and think of one specific time an AI tool told you
> something you'd already concluded yourself. Don't tell me about it yet. Just find one."

Silence. Wait. **Do not fill it.** If they cannot find one, the eligibility screen failed and the
interview should be ended courteously — see `SAMPLING_AND_RECRUITMENT.md` §2.

---

## The body

### A · The occasion — textural *(Q1, ~20 min)*

Get one concrete episode before any generalisation.

- "Take me through that one. Where were you, what were you working on?"
- "What had you concluded, before the tool said anything?"
- "How sure were you at that point?"
- "Then what did the tool say?"
- "What was the next thing you did?"
- **"What was it like to see it agree with you?"** ← the phenomenological question. Sit with it.
- "What did you notice yourself thinking?"
- "Did anything change about how the workpaper got written up?"

### B · The comparison — subquestion 7, the hinge *(~10 min)*

- "Think about the prior-year conclusion on a recurring engagement. Is seeing that the same as seeing
  the AI's answer, or different?"
- "Say more about the difference." *(Never supply one.)*
- "If you had to explain to a new associate why one deserves more scrutiny than the other — could you?"

### C · Confirmation and corroboration *(~10 min)*

- "When the tool agrees with you, is that evidence?"
- "Is it different evidence from a colleague agreeing with you?"
- "Has there been a time you went and checked anyway? What made that one different?"

### D · Stopping *(~8 min)*

- "How do you know when you've looked at an AI output enough?"
- "Has that point moved since you started using these tools?"
- "Is there anyone who would notice if it moved?"

### E · Under review *(~5 min)*

- "When you review work you know was AI-assisted, does anything change in how you review it?"
- "Would you be able to tell?"

---

## Closing — 2 minutes

> "Is there anything I should have asked and didn't?"
>
> "Is there anything you've said that you'd want me to leave out?"

Then the member-check arrangement, per `TRUSTWORTHINESS.md` §1.

## Probes — the whole set

Five probes cover almost every situation. Memorise these instead of the questions.

| Probe | Use when |
|---|---|
| "Say more about that." | Always the first move |
| "What was that like?" | They gave you an opinion; you want an experience |
| "Can you give me a specific time?" | They drifted into the general |
| "What happened right after?" | The episode stopped short |
| *Silence* | Always. It is the most productive probe and the hardest to use |

---

## What is never asked

| Never | Why |
|---|---|
| "Why did you…?" | Demands justification. Produces a defence, not a description |
| "Do you think AI makes auditors less skeptical?" | Asks them to test the hypothesis. That is the researcher's job |
| "Would you say you were anchored?" | Supplies my construct and my language. Contaminates the data |
| "Don't you think that's a risk?" | Leading, and reveals the stake |
| "How often does that happen?" | Frequency claim. Phenomenology does not license it and asking invites one |
| Anything naming a client, engagement, or firm | Confidentiality, and it is not needed |

**If a leading question slips out** — and it will — flag it in the margin during transcription and
log it in `AUDIT_TRAIL.md`. The record of where I contaminated the data is part of the data.

---

## Two operating notes

**On fluency.** I speak the language and it will show. That is what makes participants open up and it
is also what makes it easy to finish their sentences. **Do not finish their sentences.** When I supply
a word, I have supplied the finding.

**On the participant who says what I want to hear.** Some will. They will describe deference exactly
as the chain predicts, in the vocabulary I gave them by asking. That moment is logged as a hazard
flag, not banked as a result. It is the researcher-side instance of the phenomenon under study.
