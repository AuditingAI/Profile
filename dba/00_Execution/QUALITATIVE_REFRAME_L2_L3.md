# From bias to L2 and L3 — the qualitative reframe

**The one-sentence version.** The dissertation model is a *variance* model — it asks how much one
thing moves another. L2 and L3 are *process* questions — they ask how something unfolds. A survey
cannot answer a process question, which is why those two links have stayed argued rather than tested.
GEB 7911 supplies the method that fits them.

*Grounding: Creswell & Poth on qualitative question construction; recent empirical work on auditor
reliance on AI. Sources at the end. Nothing here is asserted as a finding.*

## 1 · Start from the bias

| | |
|---|---|
| **The bias** | **Anchoring-and-adjustment** (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974) |
| In audit | The prior-year conclusion becomes the reference point; current-period judgment adjusts insufficiently away from it |
| Status | Established in the literature. Yasir's eleven-construct model asks which organizational interventions reduce it |
| Model type | **Variance** — 8 interventions → 2 mediators → 1 outcome, 16 hypotheses |
| Method | Cross-sectional survey, 55 Likert items, IRB-25-0462 |

Everything below is what happens when the anchor stops being a workpaper and becomes a machine.

## 2 · The chain, and what kind of question each link is

| Link | What changes | Bias or phenomenon | Question type | Method that fits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **L1** | The anchor is now system-generated — automatic, continuous, and arriving **before** the reviewer forms a view | **Automation bias** — deference to system output | **How much / how often** — magnitude | Survey or experiment. The existing instrument |
| **L2** | The system **agrees** with a position the auditor has already stated, rather than challenging it | **Sycophancy** — a model behaviour, not a human bias | **How and through what mechanism** — process | **Interviews.** Semi-structured, experienced auditors |
| **L3** | Successive models reprocess earlier AI-influenced work; the evidentiary basis thins across engagements | **Recursive epistemic drift** — system-level, related to model collapse | **How, over what time, in what sequence** — process over time | **Longitudinal case study**, or archival across engagement years |

**The precision that matters when a professor pushes:** only **L1 involves a human cognitive bias**.
L2 is model behaviour interacting with human judgment. L3 is a property of a system of models.
Calling all three "biases" is the fastest way to lose the argument.

## 3 · How qualitative questions are actually asked

Creswell's rules, which Dr. Gonzalez's course runs on:

- **One or two central questions, then five to seven subquestions**
- **Open with "how" or "what"** — never "why", which implies a cause-and-effect agenda
- **Exploratory verbs:** explore, understand, discover, describe, report, reflect
- **Never directional verbs:** affect, influence, impact, determine, cause, relate
- **Broad and non-directional**, so participants can explain their own ideas
- **Expect them to change.** Continual review and reformulation is the design, not a failure

### The same link, asked both ways

| | |
|---|---|
| ❌ **Quantitative phrasing** | *"Does AI-generated confirmation reduce professional skepticism in audit review?"* |
| | Directional verb, yes/no answer, presumes the effect exists and only measures it |
| ✅ **Qualitative phrasing** | *"How do experienced auditors describe and account for their reliance on an AI-generated conclusion that agrees with a position they had already formed?"* |
| | Exploratory, non-directional, opens the mechanism rather than sizing an effect |

## 4 · The research questions

### L2 — sycophantic confirmation

**Central question**

> **How do experienced auditors describe and account for their reliance on AI-generated conclusions
> when the system's output agrees with a position they have already formed?**

**Subquestions**

1. What do auditors describe as happening when an AI tool confirms a judgment they had already reached?
2. How do auditors distinguish, in their own accounts, between **confirmation** and **corroboration**?
3. What practices do auditors describe using to test an AI-generated conclusion they agree with?
4. How do auditors describe the point at which they stop examining an AI output?
5. What do auditors report about how their reliance has changed across successive engagements?
6. How do reviewers describe evaluating work they know was AI-assisted?
7. What distinctions do auditors draw between an AI conclusion and a prior-year workpaper as a
   starting point?

*Subquestion 7 is the hinge — it connects the new work directly to the validated anchoring model.*

### L3 — recursive epistemic drift

**Central question**

> **How does the evidentiary basis of an audit judgment change, in auditors' accounts, across
> successive engagements in which AI-assisted work is carried forward and reprocessed?**

**Subquestions**

1. What do auditors describe carrying forward from one engagement year to the next?
2. How do auditors describe establishing where a carried-forward conclusion originally came from?
3. What do auditors report noticing when successive periods produce converging conclusions?
4. How do firms describe documenting the provenance of AI-assisted work?
5. What do auditors describe as the point at which a conclusion stops being re-examined?

## 5 · Why this design is feasible when the survey was not

This is the argument that turns the failed study into the reason for the new one.

| | Survey | Interview study |
|---|---|---|
| Required n | **100** | **15–20** |
| Eligible in the panel | ~20 of 334,976 — about six per hundred thousand | ~20 |
| Verdict | **Impossible** | **Sufficient** |

The same twenty people who made the quantitative design unworkable make a qualitative design
workable. That is not a consolation prize — it is the correct method for the question, arrived at
by way of an expensive lesson.

**Already on the record, before the course began.** The screening log of 22 July states the
open-text responses were *"genuinely rich and audit-literate — worth a short qualitative synthesis
regardless of what quantitative path is chosen."*

## 6 · The two papers from Week 1 are the two methods

Worth saying out loud to Dr. Gonzalez — it shows the seminar reading is doing work, not decorating.

| Paper | What it supplies |
|---|---|
| **Gioia & Chittipeddi (1991)** | How to build theory from accounts of meaning-making, with first-order informant terms held apart from second-order constructs — and the insider/outsider design for managing researcher bias. **This is the L2 method.** |
| **Maznevski & Chudoba (2000)** | How to see a pattern that only exists across time, by making the *interaction incident* the unit of analysis rather than the person or the meeting. **This is the L3 method** — substitute the engagement year for the incident. |

## 7 · Where the literature currently sits

Preliminary and to be verified against the FIU library before citing:

- Empirical work on AI and auditor skepticism is emerging but **mostly survey-based**. One 2024
  study of 107 external auditors in France reports a positive association between AI reliance and
  professional skepticism, moderated by trait skepticism.
- Reliance appears **contingent on task complexity**, uncertainty toward estimates, and perceptions
  of algorithmic flexibility.
- Reviews note that most work remains **conceptual, ethical, or technology-potential** in character,
  and that empirical evidence on how AI actually affects auditors is limited.
- **The gap:** little qualitative empirical work on auditors' lived experience of automation bias and
  skepticism in AI-assisted settings — interviews and observation rather than instruments.

**That gap is the opening.** It is not a claim that nobody has looked; it is that the field has been
sizing an effect it has not yet described.
