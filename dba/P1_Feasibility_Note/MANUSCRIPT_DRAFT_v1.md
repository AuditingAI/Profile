## Abstract

Online participant panels have become the default sampling frame for behavioural research, including
research on professional judgment. Their marketing emphasises panel size, and researchers budget
accordingly: a panel of several hundred thousand appears to make a target of one hundred
respondents a question of price. This note reports a case in which that inference failed completely.
A funded study of anchoring bias in auditor judgment applied three eligibility criteria — United
States residence, a current or recent audit role, and experience with multi-period recurring
engagements — to a commercial panel of 334,976 registered participants. The platform's own
eligibility engine returned approximately twenty matching participants: a prevalence of roughly six
per hundred thousand. After several weeks of two-channel fielding against a funded target of one
hundred, twenty-three responses were recorded and four survived screening. The constraint was
neither incentive level nor instrument design: effective compensation ran near $28 per hour, well
above typical academic-survey rates, and a substantial fraction of the eligible pool did engage.
The constraint was arithmetic. No budget fills one hundred seats from a pool of twenty. I argue that
panel size is close to uninformative for specialist populations, that the relevant quantity is
*eligible* prevalence under the study's own screeners, and that this quantity is observable before
any money is committed. I propose a pre-fielding prevalence check and report the specific figures so
that other researchers can calibrate against them.

**Keywords:** hard-to-reach populations; online panels; survey feasibility; auditor judgment;
sampling frames; research infrastructure

## 1. Introduction

A researcher designing a survey of a specialist profession now faces an apparently solved problem.
Commercial participant panels advertise pools in the hundreds of thousands, offer prescreening on
occupation and industry, and integrate directly with survey software. Recruitment, on this picture,
is a procurement question: choose a per-response price, fund the study, and wait.

This note reports a case in which that picture was wrong by roughly two orders of magnitude, and
argues that the error is structural rather than particular to this study.

The study in question was a validation study of an eleven-construct measurement model of anchoring
bias in auditor judgment, approved under institutional review and fielded in mid-2026 with a funded
target of one hundred usable responses. It did not reach that target. It did not come near it. What
makes the failure worth reporting is not that it happened — underpowered studies are common and
rarely interesting — but that the platform itself disclosed, in a figure visible on the study
configuration screen, exactly why it was going to happen. The eligibility engine reported that
**twenty participants out of 334,976 matched the study's screeners**. That figure was available
before fielding began. It was not consulted, because nothing in prevailing practice suggests it
should be.

That is the contribution. The number that determines whether a specialist study is feasible is not
panel size, and it is not incentive level. It is eligible prevalence under the study's own criteria,
and it is knowable in advance and free to check.

Section 2 situates the problem in the literature on hard-to-reach and elite populations. Section 3
describes the study and its eligibility criteria. Section 4 reports the prevalence finding. Section
5 rules out the two explanations a reader will reach for first — that the incentive was too low, or
that the instrument was at fault. Section 6 proposes a pre-fielding protocol. Section 7 states the
limits of what a single case can establish.

## 2. Background

Survey methodology has a long-standing vocabulary for populations that resist standard sampling.
*Hard-to-reach* populations are those for which no adequate sampling frame exists, or for which
contact is impeded by geography, stigma, or mobility. *Elite* populations — senior professionals,
executives, specialists — present a different difficulty: they are enumerable in principle but
scarce, time-poor, and weakly incentivised by the compensation levels academic budgets support.

The methodological responses to these problems are well developed. Respondent-driven sampling and
snowball designs address frames that cannot be enumerated. Professional-association partnerships,
conference intercepts, and firm-level access agreements address elite populations by borrowing an
institution's standing. Each of these approaches carries acknowledged costs in generalisability and
each is slower than panel recruitment.

Online panels entered this landscape with a different proposition: scale substituting for strategy.
If the pool is large enough and the screeners are precise enough, the argument runs, a rare
population can be reached by filtration rather than by cultivation. For many populations this works.
The literature on panel data quality is substantial and has largely converged on the view that
panel-recruited samples are adequate for a wide range of behavioural research, with attention to
attention checks, duplicate detection, and non-naivety.

What that literature has examined less closely is the **denominator**. Panel-quality research asks
whether the participants a study obtains are good ones. It asks less often whether the participants
a study *needs* exist on the panel at all. For general-population studies the question does not
arise. For studies of a specific profession, defined by role and by tenure, it is the only question
that matters — and this note reports what happens when it goes unasked.

## 3. The case

### 3.1 Design and eligibility

The study tested a measurement model of anchoring bias in recurring audit engagements: eight
organisational interventions hypothesised to disrupt anchoring, operating through two mediating
mechanisms toward one behavioural outcome, across fifty-five Likert items and sixteen hypotheses.
The design called for exploratory factor analysis followed by confirmatory factor analysis and
structural modelling, and the target of one hundred usable responses was set against
factor-analytic sample-size guidance for a model of that size.

Three eligibility screeners defined the population:

1. **Residence** — currently based in the United States;
2. **Role** — currently holding, or having held within twenty-four months, an audit role;
3. **Engagement type** — experience with continuing, multi-period engagements, that is, auditing the
   same client or entity across more than one cycle.

Two further screeners captured consent and an attestation of accurate self-report. Two attention
checks were embedded in the instrument.

Each criterion is defensible in isolation, and each follows directly from the construct. Anchoring
in recurring engagements is not measurable in a respondent who has never conducted one; the
prior-period figure that constitutes the anchor does not exist for a first-time engagement. The
United States restriction followed the approved protocol's regulatory framing.

**The criteria were not individually unreasonable. They were jointly annihilating.** That
distinction is the methodological point, and it is invisible when criteria are evaluated one at a
time, as they usually are during instrument design.

### 3.2 Fielding

The study was fielded through two channels: a commercial participant panel (Prolific) with a funded
allocation for one hundred completions at $6.00 each, and a second recruitment platform
(CloudResearch) configured in parallel. Total financial commitment across platform fees and
participant compensation was approximately $1,000. Fielding ran from late June to late July 2026,
following a pilot.

The instrument was verified end-to-end on desktop and mobile before launch, carried the
IRB-approved consent letter, and used platform-native protections against duplicate and automated
responses.

### 3.3 Outcome

Twenty-three responses were recorded across the full fielding window. Applying the pre-registered
cleaning protocol in order — removal of researcher previews and self-identified test entries,
removal of sessions abandoned before the substantive item blocks, removal of nominal completions
with blank substantive blocks, application of the five eligibility screeners, and application of
both attention checks — left **four usable responses**.

Two features of that residue are worth recording, because they bear directly on the diagnosis in
Section 5. First, **no complete and eligible respondent failed either attention check**; the
platform's own response-quality index scored the collected data at 95%. Second, every usable
respondent supplied a substantive, experience-grounded open-text account of anchoring in their own
work. The people who arrived were exactly the right people, and they answered carefully. There were
simply almost none of them.

## 4. The prevalence finding

The decisive datum was not produced by the study. It was produced by the platform, and displayed on
the study configuration screen once the screeners were entered:

> **Eligible participants: 20 of 334,976.**

Approximately **six eligible participants per one hundred thousand panel members**, or 0.006%.

Three implications follow immediately.

**The study was never fillable.** A target of one hundred usable responses required, at minimum, one
hundred eligible participants. Twenty existed. Even perfect conversion of the entire eligible
population — every one of them seeing the study, accepting it, completing it, and passing every
attention check — would have delivered one fifth of the target, before any attrition at all. The
observed yield of four usable responses represents roughly a **twenty per cent conversion of the
entire eligible universe**, which is, in isolation, a respectable recruitment performance. It is
simply performance against a denominator that made the design impossible.

**Waiting could not have helped.** Extended fielding windows address slow accrual. They do not
address exhausted eligibility. Once the eligible pool has been shown the study, additional time
adds only the trickle of newly-registering panelists who happen to match — a rate that, at six per
hundred thousand, is negligible over any horizon a doctoral timeline can accommodate.

**Price could not have helped.** This is developed in the next section, but the arithmetic is stated
here: raising per-response compensation increases the *conversion rate* within an eligible pool. It
does not increase the pool. Doubling the incentive against twenty eligible participants cannot
produce more than twenty responses.

## 5. Ruling out the alternatives

Two explanations present themselves before the prevalence explanation, and both are wrong here. It
is worth showing why, because the diagnostic reasoning generalises even where the specific figures
do not.

### 5.1 It was not the incentive

The per-response payment was $6.00 for an instrument averaging well under fifteen minutes. The
platform's own metrics reported an effective reward of approximately **$28 per hour** — substantially
above both the platform's minimum and the rates typical of academic survey research.

More decisively: incentive level and eligible prevalence are independent quantities. Compensation
operates on the decision of an eligible participant to accept a study. It has no effect whatever on
whether a participant satisfies the screeners. A study can pay any amount and still be filtered
against a pool of twenty.

The diagnostic signature of an incentive problem is a large eligible pool with low take-up. This
study displayed the opposite: a minute eligible pool with high take-up and high data quality.

### 5.2 It was not the instrument

Three observations argue against instrument failure. The instrument was verified end-to-end before
launch. Among complete and eligible respondents, the attention-check failure rate was zero and the
platform's response-quality index stood at 95%. And every usable respondent produced substantive,
domain-fluent open-text material.

Abandonment did occur — most non-completions terminated early, before the substantive blocks. But
early termination is the expected signature of *screen-out*, not of instrument fatigue: a
respondent who fails an eligibility screener placed at the front of an instrument exits at the
front of the instrument. That pattern is consistent with, and indeed predicted by, the prevalence
explanation.

### 5.3 What it was

The population, as operationalised, had a prevalence on the commercial panel of approximately six
per hundred thousand. Every other explanation is downstream of that.

## 6. What follows for practice

### 6.1 A pre-fielding prevalence check

The single recommendation of this note is procedural, free, and takes minutes.

**Before committing funds, enter the study's actual screeners into the panel's configuration
interface and record the eligible count the platform returns.** Treat that number, not the panel
size, as the feasibility constraint. Then apply a simple test:

> If eligible participants < 3 × target sample size, the study is not fillable on that panel.

The factor of three is deliberately crude and reflects the compounding of exposure, acceptance,
completion, and screening attrition. Researchers with channel-specific conversion data should
substitute their own. The point is not the constant; the point is that the comparison is made at all,
against the eligible count rather than the panel count, and made *before* money moves.

Had this check been run on the present study, it would have returned 20 against a target of 100 and
failed the test by a factor of fifteen. The design would have been revised at no cost.

### 6.2 Report eligible prevalence, not panel size

Method sections routinely report the panel used and the number of respondents obtained. They rarely
report the eligible denominator, with the result that the field accumulates no shared knowledge of
which professional populations are actually reachable and at what prevalence.

This note therefore reports its own figure in the form other researchers can use: **U.S.-based
auditors with recent role tenure and recurring multi-period engagement experience: approximately 20
of 334,976 on a major commercial panel, mid-2026 — roughly 6 per 100,000.** If a handful of studies
reported comparable figures for their own populations, feasibility could be assessed from the
literature rather than discovered by expenditure.

### 6.3 Design criteria jointly, not severally

Each of the three screeners here was individually defensible. Their conjunction was fatal. Because
criteria are typically justified one at a time during instrument development, the multiplicative
effect on prevalence is easy to miss.

A practical discipline: after finalising screeners, enter them into the panel interface
**cumulatively**, recording the eligible count after each addition. This reveals which criterion is
carrying the reduction. In the present case the interaction of role and engagement-type was almost
certainly responsible, and a widened role definition — risk, audit, compliance, or controls
professionals conducting recurring multi-period reviews — would plausibly raise prevalence by one to
two orders of magnitude while preserving the construct logic, since the anchoring mechanism requires
recurring review rather than the audit designation specifically.

### 6.4 Budget the alternative before the panel fails

Where the prevalence check fails, the established approaches to elite populations — professional
association partnerships, firm-level access agreements, conference intercepts, targeted
professional-network outreach — are not fallbacks to be improvised mid-fielding. They require lead
time measured in months: institutional contact, reciprocal value, sometimes protocol amendment.

The prevalence check is valuable precisely because it delivers that verdict early enough for those
timelines to be met.

## 7. Limitations

This is a single case on a single pair of platforms at a single point in time, and every specific
figure should be read accordingly.

The prevalence figure is platform-reported and its internal derivation is not observable; it may
reflect participants' self-reported profile data rather than verified employment, and profile data
may be stale. If anything this biases the reported figure **upward** — some of the twenty may not
have satisfied the criteria on examination — which strengthens rather than weakens the argument.

Prevalence is time-varying and platform-varying. A different panel, or the same panel in a different
year, would return a different number. That is not a limitation of the recommendation; it is the
reason the check must be run per study rather than inferred from prior work.

The study restricted to United States residence, and that restriction contributed to the reduction.
Studies without a jurisdictional constraint will find larger eligible pools. The multiplicative
logic is unaffected.

Finally, this note establishes that one specialist population was unreachable at a specific
prevalence. It does not establish a general prevalence for professional populations on commercial
panels — no single case could. What it establishes is that the gap between panel size and eligible
count can be large enough to make a fully funded, competently executed, ethically approved study
impossible, and that this is discoverable in advance for no cost.

## 8. Conclusion

A study was funded, approved, competently instrumented, and fielded through two channels at
above-market compensation. It failed, and the reason was visible on the configuration screen before
any of that happened: twenty eligible participants out of 334,976.

The lesson is not that panels are inadequate. For a wide range of research they are excellent, and
for this population the platform behaved exactly as designed — it correctly reported that almost
nobody matched. The lesson is that panel size is close to meaningless for specialist populations,
that the operative quantity is eligible prevalence under the study's own screeners, and that
researchers are not currently in the habit of looking at it.

The remedy costs nothing. Enter the screeners. Read the number. Compare it to the target before
comparing it to the budget.
